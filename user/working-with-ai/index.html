Working with AI

Prepare well, keep your work portable, and stay in control of quality.

Context Matters

The quality of what an AI produces depends on what you give it to work with. An important aspect of using a generative AI tool is what you prepare before you prompt.

Context engineering is the practice of assembling, structuring, and refining the information you bring into an AI conversation so the tool can do something useful with it. For educators, this usually means:

  • the task you want help with, stated specifically enough that you would know what to look for in the result
  • the audience: who this is for, what they already know, where they tend to struggle
  • source material: a rubric, a set of learning outcomes, a reading, sample student work (de-identified), or notes from a consultation
  • constraints: format, length, tone, and any boundaries on what the output should include
  • your success criteria: what would make the result worth using, and what would make you set it aside

Not every task needs all of these. What you include depends on your goal and what the task requires.

This collection of materials and instructions is your context packet. A well-prepared context packet improves your first result, speeds up iteration, and makes your judgment easier to apply.

The experiment planner on Run an Experiment walks you through building a context packet. Filling in stages 1 and 2 gives you most of what you need.

Conversations Are Temporary

AI conversations have a finite working memory. As a conversation gets longer, the tool may lose track of earlier instructions, produce more generic output, or require you to re-explain things you already covered. Conversations can also end at any time. The tool may time out, you may hit a usage limit, or you may want to switch tools.

Your thinking stays with you. Save your work as you go. If a conversation ends or stops being useful, upload your context into a new session and continue from there.

Signs it is time to start a new conversation:

  • the tool ignores constraints you set earlier
  • outputs get generic or repetitive
  • you find yourself re-explaining the same thing
  • you are spending more time correcting than generating

Some tools let you save standing instructions that persist across conversations (for example, ChatGPT Projects or Claude Projects). These help with recurring tasks but are optional.

Treat each conversation like a single work session. One task, one focused conversation.

Staying in Control

Working with AI is an iterative process. Your judgment is involved at every stage. The guidance below describes what careful practice looks like.

Before you run the experiment

Get specific about what you are trying to do. Define what you are looking for in the result, and decide in advance what would make you stop or change direction. Keep the scope to one task and one context.

If source material is involved, de-identify student or personal information before pasting it into a tool. If learners will interact with the output, make your expectations about AI use explicit.

While you work

AI output can look polished and still contain errors. Keep your own judgment front and centre on educational and evaluative decisions. The tool can draft. You decide what is appropriate for your students.

As you work, keep track of what you are keeping, editing, and rejecting, and why. If you are spending more time correcting the output than doing the work yourself, the task may not be a good fit for AI, or your context packet may need more detail.

Pause if you notice: factual errors presented confidently, output that does not fit your pedagogical intent, unclear boundaries for student-facing use, or bias in framing or representation.

After the run

Record what happened while it is fresh. Note what worked and what did not. Pay attention to which decisions in your context packet or prompts influenced the result. Decide whether to continue, modify your approach, or stop. If you are running a sandbox experiment, document your reasoning.

What to Check

Before using any AI-generated output in your teaching, advising, or support work, review it against these questions:

  • Does it align with the learning or workflow goal you started with?
  • Is it factually and procedurally accurate?
  • Is it clear and appropriate for the intended audience?
  • Is the language accessible and inclusive?
  • Does it align with relevant policies on academic integrity and AI use?

These cover the areas where AI output most often needs correction.

Warning Signs

Some problems with AI output are easy to miss because the surface quality is high. Watch for:

  • Confident inaccuracy. The output states something wrong with confidence. Always check against your own expertise.
  • Polish without substance. The language reads well but the content does not serve the pedagogical goal. This often happens when the context packet was too vague.
  • Unclear student-facing boundaries. The output could go to students, but it does not make clear what role AI played or what students are expected to do with it independently.
  • Diminishing returns. You have been iterating and the output is not improving. Stop, rethink your approach, or do the task yourself.
