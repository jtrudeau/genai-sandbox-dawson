23:T24ea,# After the Workshop: Your Next Steps

**You just completed the [90-Minute Sandbox Workshop](/workshop-in-a-box/workshop-90min).** Well done!

You experienced using AI as a thoughtful tool, generated experiment ideas, and heard how colleagues are approaching this work. Now what?

This page guides you from workshop insights to actual experiments in your teaching.

---

## What You Have Right Now

From today's workshop, you should have:

- ✅ **5+ experiment ideas** from the first brainstorming activity (Set 2 prompt)
- ✅ **1 deeper scenario exploration** from the second activity (Set 3 prompt)
- ✅ **Notes from small group discussions** - what resonated, what concerns came up
- ✅ **Connections with colleagues** exploring similar questions

**If you didn't capture these:** No problem. You can revisit the [Workshop Prompts](/workshop-in-a-box/workshop-prompts) and run through the activities again on your own.

---

## This Week: Reflect & Choose

**Don't rush into action.** Take time to reflect on what emerged.

### Reflection Questions (5-10 minutes)

1. **Which idea felt most doable?**
   - Not perfect, not transformative - just doable
   - What makes it feel achievable?

2. **Which idea felt most relevant to your current teaching challenges?**
   - What problem would this help you address?
   - Why does it matter now?

3. **What concerns or questions are you sitting with?**
   - Ethical considerations?
   - Technical uncertainties?
   - Student impact questions?

4. **What would make you more confident to try something?**
   - More examples?
   - A partner to experiment with?
   - Specific guidance on a tool or approach?

### Choose ONE Experiment

From your workshop notes, **pick one experiment idea** to develop further.

**How to choose:**
- Start with the smallest, most reversible version
- Pick something you can try in the next 2-4 weeks
- Choose something that excites your curiosity (not just efficiency)
- Select an idea where you're genuinely uncertain about the outcome

**Make it smaller:**
- Can you try this with just one class session instead of a whole unit?
- Can you test it with 5 students before the whole class?
- Can you prototype just one piece of the workflow first?

---

## Next 2-4 Weeks: Design Your Experiment

Now that you've chosen an idea, it's time to develop it into a structured experiment.

### Use the Experiment Planner

→ **[Experiment Planner Template](/user/guides/experiment-planner)**

This interactive template helps you document:

**1. Exploration Goal Snapshot**
- What are you trying to learn?
- Why does this matter for your teaching?
- What risks or ethical considerations exist?

**2. Workflow & Experiment Log**
- Step-by-step process: AI assist + Human judgment
- What will you actually do?
- How will AI and human decision-making work together?

**3. Outputs & Artifacts**
- What will you create or produce?
- How will you capture evidence?

**4. Reflection Check-In**
- What shifted during the experiment?
- How did AI help or hinder?
- What will you keep, change, or try next?

**5. Verification Snapshot**
- How will you verify AI outputs?
- What quality checks will you use?

**6. Next Experiment Hooks**
- What would you try next?
- What support would help?

### Learn from Examples

Not sure how to structure your experiment? Browse examples similar to your context:

→ **[Faculty Examples](/user/examples/faculty)**

**Available examples:**
- **[Arduino Robotics Code Review](/user/examples/faculty/arduino-robotics)** - AI-assisted code feedback for Arduino sketches
- **[Anthropology Word-Weaving](/user/examples/faculty/anthropology)** - In-class creativity + AI as reflective partner
- **[Industrial Design](/user/examples/faculty/industrial-design)** - AI co-designer workflows

Each example includes:
- Clear teaching goal and context
- Step-by-step workflows
- AI tool specifications

- Verification and reflection prompts

**How to use examples:**
1. Find the closest match to your discipline or goal
2. Skim the workflow to see how AI and human judgment are paired
3. Adapt the structure to your specific experiment
4. Use the verification patterns for quality assurance

---

## Running Your Experiment

### Before You Start

**1. Plan transparency with students**

How will you tell students about this experiment?

Example language:
> "I'm trying something new this week. I used AI to help generate [discussion prompts/feedback templates/practice problems], and I reviewed and edited them to make sure they fit our learning goals. I want to see if this helps [improve discussion quality/provide faster feedback/offer more practice]. Let me know how it goes for you."

**2. Decide what evidence to collect**

- Screenshots of AI outputs?
- Notes during class about student engagement?
- Student feedback on the experience?
- Your own reflections after each session?

**3. Set your experiment timeline**

- When will you try this?
- How long will you run it?
- When will you pause to reflect?

Your facilitator may have set a timeline for the cohort (e.g., end-of-semester check-in). Align your experiment to that timeline if applicable.

### During Your Experiment

**Document as you go:**
- What prompts did you use?
- What outputs did AI generate?
- What did you keep, modify, or reject?
- What surprised you?
- What worked or didn't work?

Use the **Workflow & Experiment Log** section of the [Experiment Planner](/user/guides/experiment-planner) to capture this in real-time.

**Stay flexible:**
- If something isn't working, adjust or stop
- If something unexpected emerges, explore it
- This is a sandbox - you can change direction

### After Your Experiment

**Complete the reflection sections** in the Experiment Planner:
- What shifted during the sandbox run?
- How did AI help or hinder your approach?
- What will you keep, change, or try next?

**Decide what's next:**
- Continue this experiment with modifications?
- Try a different experiment?
- Stop and reflect on what you learned?
- Share your findings with colleagues?

---

## End-of-Semester: Share What You Learned

Your facilitator may organize an **optional cohort check-in** at the end of the semester.

This is a chance to:
- Share what you tried (successes, failures, and in-betweens)
- Hear what colleagues experimented with
- Identify patterns and insights across experiments
- Decide what to continue, modify, or stop
- Support each other in refining approaches

**What to bring to the check-in:**
- Your completed Experiment Planner (or notes from your experiment)
- 1-2 artifacts or examples from your work
- Honest observations about what happened
- Questions or challenges you're still working through

**If there's no formal check-in:**
- Find a colleague who also participated and share informally
- Bring your experiment to a department meeting
- Document your findings for your own future reference

---

## You Don't Have to Do This Alone

### Options for Continued Support

**Option 1: Partner with a colleague**
- Pair up with someone from today's workshop
- Check in monthly to share what you're trying
- Provide feedback and encouragement

**Option 2: Department-level exploration**
- Bring this approach back to your department
- Run experiments in parallel, share findings
- Build discipline-specific best practices

**Option 3: Stay connected with the cohort**
- Participate in optional check-ins
- Share experiments and troubleshoot challenges
- Build a community of practice

**Option 4: Work solo**
- Use the resources here to guide your work
- Document your experiments for future reference
- Share when you're ready

## Three Reminders

As you move forward:

**1. Permission to start small**

You don't need to have it all figured out. One small experiment. One class session. That's enough.

**2. Permission to fail**

If your experiment doesn't work, that's not failure—that's data. Document what you learned and try something different.

**3. Permission to say no**

Not every experiment will be worth continuing. Not every AI tool will serve your teaching. You get to decide based on what you observe, not what others say you should do.

94:T24ea,# After the Workshop: Your Next Steps

**You just completed the [90-Minute Sandbox Workshop](/workshop-in-a-box/workshop-90min).** Well done!

You experienced using AI as a thoughtful tool, generated experiment ideas, and heard how colleagues are approaching this work. Now what?

This page guides you from workshop insights to actual experiments in your teaching.

---

## What You Have Right Now

From today's workshop, you should have:

- ✅ **5+ experiment ideas** from the first brainstorming activity (Set 2 prompt)
- ✅ **1 deeper scenario exploration** from the second activity (Set 3 prompt)
- ✅ **Notes from small group discussions** - what resonated, what concerns came up
- ✅ **Connections with colleagues** exploring similar questions

**If you didn't capture these:** No problem. You can revisit the [Workshop Prompts](/workshop-in-a-box/workshop-prompts) and run through the activities again on your own.

---

## This Week: Reflect & Choose

**Don't rush into action.** Take time to reflect on what emerged.

### Reflection Questions (5-10 minutes)

1. **Which idea felt most doable?**
   - Not perfect, not transformative - just doable
   - What makes it feel achievable?

2. **Which idea felt most relevant to your current teaching challenges?**
   - What problem would this help you address?
   - Why does it matter now?

3. **What concerns or questions are you sitting with?**
   - Ethical considerations?
   - Technical uncertainties?
   - Student impact questions?

4. **What would make you more confident to try something?**
   - More examples?
   - A partner to experiment with?
   - Specific guidance on a tool or approach?

### Choose ONE Experiment

From your workshop notes, **pick one experiment idea** to develop further.

**How to choose:**
- Start with the smallest, most reversible version
- Pick something you can try in the next 2-4 weeks
- Choose something that excites your curiosity (not just efficiency)
- Select an idea where you're genuinely uncertain about the outcome

**Make it smaller:**
- Can you try this with just one class session instead of a whole unit?
- Can you test it with 5 students before the whole class?
- Can you prototype just one piece of the workflow first?

---

## Next 2-4 Weeks: Design Your Experiment

Now that you've chosen an idea, it's time to develop it into a structured experiment.

### Use the Experiment Planner

→ **[Experiment Planner Template](/user/guides/experiment-planner)**

This interactive template helps you document:

**1. Exploration Goal Snapshot**
- What are you trying to learn?

- Step-by-step process: AI assist + Human judgment
- What will you actually do?
- How will AI and human decision-making work together?

**3. Outputs & Artifacts**
- What will you create or produce?
- How will you capture evidence?

**4. Reflection Check-In**
- What shifted during the experiment?
- How did AI help or hinder?

**5. Verification Snapshot**
- How will you verify AI outputs?
- What quality checks will you use?

**6. Next Experiment Hooks**
- What would you try next?

## Running Your Experiment

### Before You Start

**1. Plan transparency with students**

How will you tell students about this experiment?

Example language:
> "I'm trying something new this week. I used AI to help generate [discussion prompts/feedback templates/practice problems], and I reviewed and edited them to make sure they fit our learning goals. I want to see if this helps [improve discussion quality/provide faster feedback/offer more practice]. Let me know how it goes for you."

**2. Decide what evidence to collect**

- Screenshots of AI outputs?
- Notes during class about student engagement?
- Student feedback on the experience?
- Your own reflections after each session?

**3. Set your experiment timeline**

- When will you try this?
- How long will you run it?
- When will you pause to reflect?

Your facilitator may have set a timeline for the cohort (e.g., end-of-semester check-in). Align your experiment to that timeline if applicable.

### During Your Experiment

**Document as you go:**
- What prompts did you use?
- What outputs did AI generate?
- What did you keep, modify, or reject?
- What surprised you?
- What worked or didn't work?

Use the **Workflow & Experiment Log** section of the [Experiment Planner](/user/guides/experiment-planner) to capture this in real-time.

**Stay flexible:**
- If something isn't working, adjust or stop
- If something unexpected emerges, explore it
- This is a sandbox - you can change direction

### After Your Experiment

**Complete the reflection sections** in the Experiment Planner:
- What shifted during the sandbox run?
- How did AI help or hinder your approach?
- What will you keep, change, or try next?

**Decide what's next:**
- Continue this experiment with modifications?
- Try a different experiment?
- Stop and reflect on what you learned?
- Share your findings with colleagues?

## End-of-Semester: Share What You Learned

Your facilitator may organize an **optional cohort check-in** at the end of the semester.

This is a chance to:
- Share what you tried (successes, failures, and in-betweens)
- Hear what colleagues experimented with
- Identify patterns and insights across experiments
- Decide what to continue, modify, or stop
- Support each other in refining approaches

**What to bring to the check-in:**
- Your completed Experiment Planner (or notes from your experiment)
- 1-2 artifacts or examples from your work
- Honest observations about what happened
- Questions or challenges you're still working through

**If there's no formal check-in:**
- Find a colleague who also participated and share informally
- Bring your experiment to a department meeting
- Document your findings for your own future reference

## Three Reminders

As you move forward:

**1. Permission to start small**

You don't need to have it all figured out. One small experiment. One class session. That's enough.

**2. Permission to fail**

If your experiment doesn't work, that's not failure—that's data. Document what you learned and try something different.

**3. Permission to say no**

Not every experiment will be worth continuing. Not every AI tool will serve your teaching. You get to decide based on what you observe, not what others say you should do.
