Places to Start

Use this page when you want a first experiment, not a long list to browse. Each starting point is for one person working through a specific task.

Choose one that feels close to your work, then decide:

  • what the first use will be
  • what you are bringing
  • what you will look for

Pick one, make it specific enough to review, and continue with Run an Experiment.

Jump to: Teaching | Support | Working with a Team

Teaching and Course Work

Clarify difficult concepts on one topic

Teaching
  • Situation: Students can use formulas, terms, or procedures but struggle to explain what they mean.
  • Possible first experiment: Draft 3 concept-check questions for one topic and compare them with what you already use.
  • What you might bring: one topic, your current questions or notes, and the kind of explanation you want from students.
  • What to look for: clearer reasoning, not just recall, and no new confusion about the topic.

Improve discussion questions for one reading or class

Teaching
  • Situation: Discussion stays at summary level.
  • Possible first experiment: Draft 3 discussion questions for one reading, case, or class topic.
  • What you might bring: the reading or case, your current prompt, and the kind of thinking you want students to show.
  • What to look for: more comparison, evidence, and explanation, without flattening nuance or inviting generic answers.

Support revision on one writing task

Teaching
  • Situation: Students need help revising a draft, but feedback often comes too late or stays too broad.
  • Possible first experiment: Draft first-pass feedback prompts or revision questions for one writing task.
  • What you might bring: the assignment, rubric, common weaknesses, and one sample paragraph or excerpt if needed.
  • What to look for: stronger second drafts in the area you targeted, with feedback that still fits the assignment.

Clarify assignment language or rubric criteria

Teaching
  • Situation: Students misread assignment instructions or rubric language.
  • Possible first experiment: Rewrite one small part of an assignment or two rubric criteria in clearer language.
  • What you might bring: the current instructions or rubric, common student questions, and the meaning you need to preserve.
  • What to look for: fewer clarification questions and quicker understanding, without changing the standard.

Clarify AI-use instructions for one assignment

Teaching
  • Situation: Students are unsure what kind of AI use is allowed for a specific assignment.
  • Possible first experiment: Draft assignment-specific AI-use instructions for one task.
  • What you might bring: the assignment, current course expectations, common student questions, and any department or institution guidance you need to respect.
  • What to look for: clearer expectations and fewer boundary questions, without making the instructions too vague or too rigid.

Add self-explanation or reflection to one activity

Teaching
  • Situation: Students complete a task but do not explain their reasoning or reflect with much depth.
  • Possible first experiment: Add 2 or 3 self-explanation or reflection prompts to one activity.
  • What you might bring: the activity, the kind of reasoning you want to see, and examples of shallow responses you want to move beyond.
  • What to look for: more specific, evidence-based responses without turning the activity into formulaic extra work.

Draft earlier feedback for one checkpoint

Teaching
  • Situation: Students need feedback earlier, before a final submission or later milestone.
  • Possible first experiment: Draft formative feedback comments or question stems for one checkpoint.
  • What you might bring: the milestone description, rubric criteria, and the most common issues you see at that stage.
  • What to look for: better revision or decision-making at the next checkpoint, without adding correction work you do not need.

Adapt one task for mixed readiness

Teaching
  • Situation: Students in the same group need different levels of support.
  • Possible first experiment: Draft two versions of one activity: one with more structure and one with more independence.
  • What you might bring: the current activity, the support you already provide, and what should stay the same across both versions.
  • What to look for: more students completing the task successfully without confusion about which version to use.

Support Work

Improve research-method support for one session or visit

Support
  • Situation: Students need help with search strategy, source choice, or evaluating information.
  • Possible first experiment: Draft one short activity or comparison task for a library visit, workshop, or support session.
  • What you might bring: your current activity, the source-quality issues you keep seeing, and the habits you want students to practice.
  • What to look for: better research questions and more careful source choices, without encouraging students to rely on unchecked summaries.

Improve one recurring follow-up message or resource

Support
  • Situation: You often repeat the same advice after consultations, appointments, or support requests.
  • Possible first experiment: Draft one follow-up message, handout section, or resource for a recurring situation.
  • What you might bring: notes from past interactions, common questions, and the points that must stay accurate.
  • What to look for: clearer wording that is easier to personalize and still captures the nuance you need.

Improve one consultation structure

Support
  • Situation: Conversations about assignments, AI use, or redesign work are useful but hard to document or carry forward.
  • Possible first experiment: Draft a one-page consultation template for one recurring kind of support conversation.
  • What you might bring: your current notes or template, the questions you usually ask, and the decisions that need to come out of the conversation.
  • What to look for: a conversation that is easier to guide and easier to follow up on, without losing discipline-specific detail.

If You Are Working with a Team

If the work involves shared AI-use language across courses, department alignment, or program-level goals, that is usually better handled as a facilitated session than as one person’s first experiment.
