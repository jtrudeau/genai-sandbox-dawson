24:T4dd7,# Anthropology Word-Weaving: Full Simulation

> **Original Assignment Credit:** This assignment is adapted from Mike Wesch's Word-Weaving exercise.
> Original assignment: [https://anth101.com/challenge4/](https://anth101.com/challenge4/)
> The adaptation described here was developed at Dawson College to address challenges when AI tools became widely available.

---

## Assignment Context

**Course:** Introduction to Anthropology (381-1N1-DW)
**Assignment:** Word-Weaving (10% of course grade)
**Class size:** ~40 students
**Format:** Part 1 in-class (4%), Part 2 take-home (6%)

**Learning goals:**
- Understand how language shapes thought and culture
- Practice metalinguistic awareness (seeing your own seeing)
- Engage with concepts from course (metaphors we live by, linguistic relativity)
- Develop cultural observation skills

---

## The Problem: AI Killed the Original Assignment

### Original Format (All Take-Home)

**Assignment:**
1. Invent a new phrase or metaphor
2. Introduce it to family/friends
3. Reflect on the experience

**What students submitted before AI:**
- Creative, personal phrases
- Thoughtful reflections on reactions
- Cultural and linguistic diversity

**What students submitted after AI became widespread:**
- Generic AI-generated phrases like "time gardening" or "emotional weather"
- Identical examples across multiple students
- Superficial reflections clearly written by ChatGPT
- No personal investment or authentic learning

**The assignment became:** "Ask ChatGPT to invent a phrase, ask it to write a reflection, submit."

### Why the Redesign Was Necessary

**The learning objectives disappeared:**
- Students weren't practicing creativity
- No genuine cultural observation
- No metalinguistic awareness
- Just copying AI output

**The assessment became meaningless:**
- Couldn't distinguish student work from AI work
- Generic responses revealed no understanding
- Grading felt like checking if they ran the prompt correctly

---

## The Redesign: Protecting the Creative Core

### New Assignment Structure

**Part 1 (4%, in-class, ~40 minutes):**
- **Step 1:** Invent your phrase/metaphor (devices stowed)
- **Step 2:** Explain its meaning and intent

**Part 2 (6%, take-home, due 1 week later):**
- **Step 3:** Test phrase with family/friends + converse with ChatGPT
- **Step 4:** Reflect on both experiences

**Key change:** The creative and explanatory work happens in class where AI can't do it for them.

---

## Part 1: In-Class Session (40 minutes)

### Preparation (Before Class)

**Materials needed:**
- Printed assignment handout (students can't access devices)
- Examples from course readings (metaphors from Lakoff & Johnson)
- Examples of creative phrases (from previous years or your own)

**Room setup:**
- Clear instruction that all devices must be stowed
- Mention this is worth 4% and due at end of class

### In-Class Facilitation (Step by Step)

#### Introduction (5 minutes)

**Instructor explains:**

> "Today we're starting the Word-Weaving assignment. This is your chance to practice something anthropologists do: observe how language shapes our thinking and create new linguistic possibilities.
>
> You'll invent a new phrase or metaphor - something that doesn't exist yet but that you think would make the world better. Then you'll test it in the real world.
>
> The first part happens right now, in class, without devices. This is your creative work - it needs to be yours."

**Show examples from course:**
- "Argument is war" → What if we said "argument is dance"?
- "Time is money" → What if we said "time is water"?
- "Falling in love" → What could we say instead?

**Mention multilingual possibilities:**
> "Many of you speak multiple languages. You might invent a phrase in another language or bring an expression from your language that doesn't exist in English. That's fantastic - just make sure to explain it to me."

#### Step 1: Invention (15-20 minutes)

**Prompt on board:**

```
Invent a phrase or metaphor that you think would make the world a better place.

Think about:
- Arguments (what if they weren't wars?)
- Love (what if it wasn't falling?)
- Education (what if it wasn't filling containers?)
- Peace, the self, relationships, success, etc.

Your invention can be:
- A new metaphor (X is Y)
- A new phrase to describe feelings or experiences
- A translation/adaptation from another language
- A playful or serious expression

Remember:
- Must be your original invention
- Consider your audience (family/friends)
- Be respectful
```

**While students work:**
- Circulate and answer questions
- Encourage the hesitant ones ("Start with something small")
- Validate multilingual ideas
- Remind them they'll have time to refine later

**Common student concerns:**
- "What if it's not good?" → "That's fine, you'll test it and reflect"
- "Can I use [phrase in another language]?" → "Yes, as long as you explain it"
- "What if no one uses it?" → "That's data for your reflection!"

#### Step 2: Explanation (15-20 minutes)

**Prompt:**

```
In 1-2 paragraphs (5-7 sentences each), explain:

1. What does your phrase/metaphor mean?
2. What do you wish to communicate with it?
3. Who do you want to use it with?
4. What inspired you to create this?
5. What reception do you expect?
```

**Students write their explanations.**

**While students work:**
- Encourage specificity ("Who exactly will you try this with?")
- Ask about inspiration ("What made you think of this?")
- Check for clarity ("Could someone understand this?")

#### Collection (5 minutes)

**Collect both parts before students leave:**
- The invented phrase/metaphor
- The 1-2 paragraph explanation

**Give Part 2 instructions:**
- Handout with Steps 3-4 details
- Due date (typically 1 week later)
- Remind them they can now use ChatGPT as a conversation partner

---

## Part 2: Take-Home with AI Assist

### Step 3: Testing & Conversation

**Instructions for students:**

> **1. Test with humans:**
> Introduce your phrase or metaphor in conversation with family or friends as if it has always existed. See if they catch on and start using it themselves. If they ask about it, give them a strong pitch for why it should exist.
>
> **2. Converse with ChatGPT:**
> Ask ChatGPT how it understands your new expression. Have a conversation about the usage of your phrase/metaphor and whether it could be improved.

**Sample ChatGPT prompts (provided to students):**

**Prompt 1: Initial Understanding**
```
I created a new phrase/metaphor: "[your phrase here]"

The idea behind it is: [brief explanation]

How do you understand this expression? What do you think it communicates?
```

**Prompt 2: Usage Exploration**
```
Based on your understanding, in what situations or contexts do you think this phrase could be used?

Do you think it effectively communicates what I intended?
```

**Prompt 3: Refinement**
```
Do you have suggestions for how this phrase could be improved or made clearer?

What are the strengths and potential weaknesses of this expression?
```

**Prompt 4: Comparison**
```
When I introduced this to my [family/friends], they [describe their reaction].

How does your understanding and reaction compare to how humans responded to it?
```

### Step 4: Reflection

**Instructions for students:**

> Write 2 paragraphs (5-7 sentences each) describing:
>
> **Paragraph 1:** What happened when you introduced the expression to family/friends? How was it received? What did you observe about their reactions?
>
> **Paragraph 2:** What did ChatGPT think about your phrase? How did its response compare to human reactions? Is there anything you would choose to improve in your phrase or metaphor? Why or why not?

---

## Student Examples

### Example 1: Multilingual Invention

**Student's phrase:** "Saudade-weaving" (Portuguese/English hybrid)

**Step 2 explanation (in-class):**
> "I grew up speaking Portuguese and English. In Portuguese, 'saudade' means a deep longing for something or someone absent - it's more than just missing someone. I created 'saudade-weaving' to describe how we build connections across distance through small acts - sending messages, sharing memories, keeping traditions alive across countries.
>
> I want to use this with my family who live in Brazil and Canada. I was inspired by how we stay connected despite the distance. I expect my family will understand because they know saudade, but my English-speaking friends might find it beautiful but hard to grasp."

**Step 3 human testing:**
> "I tried it with my mom during a video call. I said 'We're saudade-weaving right now' and she immediately smiled and understood. She said it perfectly captured what we do. My Canadian friend asked what it meant, and when I explained, she said she wished English had a word like that."

**Step 3 ChatGPT conversation (excerpt):**
> Student: "I created 'saudade-weaving' to describe building connections across distance. How do you understand it?"
>
> ChatGPT: "This is a beautiful hybrid expression. 'Saudade' carries that uniquely Portuguese sense of longing, while 'weaving' suggests active creation... it evokes the idea of maintaining bonds through intentional effort despite physical separation."

**Step 4 reflection:**
> "My family immediately understood saudade-weaving because they live it - we're constantly bridging distances. ChatGPT understood it intellectually and gave good suggestions, but my mom's reaction was emotional and real. She felt seen by the phrase.
>
> ChatGPT suggested I could make it clearer for English speakers by explaining saudade first. That's practical but kind of misses the point - some feelings don't translate neatly. I like keeping the Portuguese word because it honors that cultural specificity. I wouldn't change it."

**Why this works:**
- Personal cultural knowledge (saudade) that AI couldn't generate
- Authentic family connection and observation
- Critical reflection comparing AI's intellectual understanding vs. human emotional response
- Student owns their creative choice based on reflection

---

### Example 2: Metaphor Invention

**Student's phrase:** "Friendship gardening"

**Step 2 explanation (in-class):**
> "I invented 'friendship gardening' to describe maintaining friendships. Like plants, friendships need regular care - watering, pruning, sometimes replanting. We talk about 'working on' relationships, which sounds like effort without joy. Gardening captures both the work and the pleasure.
>
> I want to use this with close friends who I sometimes lose touch with. I was inspired by how my dad talks about his garden - patient, attentive, long-term thinking. I expect some friends will think it's cheesy, but others might find it meaningful."

**Step 3 human testing:**
> "I told my friend 'I've been bad at friendship gardening lately' and she laughed but got it immediately. She said 'Yeah, I need to water our friendship more.' We ended up having a real conversation about staying in touch. My roommate thought it was corny but admitted she understood what I meant."

**Step 3 ChatGPT conversation (excerpt):**
> ChatGPT suggested "friendship gardening" could work in contexts like "We've been doing some friendship gardening over coffee" or "This relationship needs some gardening." It recommended keeping the metaphor consistent (not mixing gardening with other metaphors). It noted the phrase might work better as a verb ("garden our friendship") than a noun.

**Step 4 reflection:**
> "My friends understood it right away and one even started using it naturally in conversation. That felt successful - it filled a gap for us. ChatGPT analyzed the grammar and usage patterns, which was helpful for thinking about how it functions linguistically.
>
> The big difference: my friends reacted emotionally and personally. ChatGPT reacted analytically. Both were useful but for different reasons. I might refine it to 'garden friendships' (verb form) based on ChatGPT's suggestion, but the core metaphor works because it resonates with lived experience, not because it's grammatically perfect."

**Why this works:**
- Student created something personally meaningful
- Observed real human reactions (emotional, conversational)
- Used AI for linguistic analysis and refinement
- Reflection shows critical thinking about different types of feedback

---

### Example 3: Phrase for Emotions

**Student's phrase:** "Holding weather"

**Step 2 explanation (in-class):**
> "I created 'holding weather' to describe sitting with someone through their emotional storm without trying to fix it. We say 'weather the storm' for ourselves, but what about being present for someone else's storm? Holding weather means you're there, steady, not fixing or fleeing.
>
> I want to use this with friends who are going through hard times. I was inspired by my sister's depression - I couldn't fix it, but I could be present. I expect people might not get it at first, but it could be useful once explained."

**Step 3 human testing:**
> "I tried it with my friend who's struggling with family stuff. I said 'I'm just here holding weather with you.' She paused and asked what I meant. When I explained, she teared up and said it was exactly what she needed - not advice, just presence. She texted me later saying she loved the phrase."

**Step 3 ChatGPT conversation (excerpt):**
> ChatGPT found "holding weather" evocative and understood the concept of supportive presence. It suggested potential confusion ("holding" might imply control) and offered alternatives like "weathering together" or "sharing the weather." It noted the phrase works because it creates visual imagery.

**Step 4 reflection:**
> "My friend's reaction was powerful - she felt understood by the phrase in a way that mattered emotionally. ChatGPT's response was more technical, analyzing the metaphor's mechanics and suggesting alternatives. Both helped me think about the phrase differently.
>
> ChatGPT's concern about 'holding' implying control is valid, but I think 'holding space' is already understood in English, so 'holding weather' builds on that. I'm keeping it as is because my friend's emotional response showed it works. The phrase isn't perfect linguistically, but it serves the purpose I intended - naming an experience that needed naming."

**Why this works:**
- Deeply personal origin (sister's depression)
- Real-world testing revealed emotional resonance
- AI provided linguistic analysis that student evaluated critically
- Student made reasoned choice to keep phrase despite AI's concerns

## Assessment Rubric

### Part 1: In-Class Creation (4%)

**Phrase/Metaphor Invention (2%)**
- ✅ Original creation (not AI-generated or widely known)
- ✅ Clear and understandable
- ✅ Thoughtfully conceived

**Explanation (2%)**
- ✅ Meaning clearly explained
- ✅ Audience and purpose identified
- ✅ Personal inspiration or reasoning shared
- ✅ Expected reception considered

**Note:** Part 1 is primarily assessed for completion and personal engagement, not perfection.

### Part 2: Take-Home Reflection (6%)

**Testing & ChatGPT Conversation (2%)**
- ✅ Evidence of real-world testing with humans
- ✅ Documented conversation with ChatGPT
- ✅ Multiple aspects of the phrase explored with AI

**Reflection Quality (4%)**
- **Depth:** Goes beyond surface-level description to analyze reactions
- **Comparison:** Meaningfully compares human and AI responses
- **Critical thinking:** Evaluates feedback and makes reasoned choices
- **Personal insight:** Shows metalinguistic awareness and cultural observation

**What we're looking for:**
- Not just "my friend liked it" but "why did they react that way?"
- Not just "ChatGPT suggested X" but "do I agree? Why or why not?"
- Evidence of genuine reflection on language, culture, and meaning
- Personal voice and engagement throughout

**Red flags (possible AI-written reflection):**
- Generic observations ("the phrase was well-received")
- No specific quotes or details from human testing
- Overly polished academic language without personal voice
- No critical evaluation of ChatGPT's suggestions

## Iteration Notes (From Instructor)

### What Worked Immediately

**In-class creation:**
- Students produced authentic, creative work
- Multilingual inventions emerged naturally
- Personal investment was visible

**AI as conversation partner:**
- Students used ChatGPT thoughtfully for analysis
- Comparisons between human and AI reactions were insightful
- Reflections showed genuine engagement

### Adjustments Made

**Providing more structure:**
- Initially students felt lost with open-ended creativity
- Added more examples from course readings
- Provided clearer constraints (categories, audience guidance)
- Result: Students engaged more confidently

**Sample ChatGPT prompts:**
- Some students didn't know how to start the AI conversation
- Provided sample prompts to scaffold the interaction
- Students still personalized them based on their phrases

**Emphasizing reflection over product:**
- Some students worried their phrases weren't "good enough"
- Clarified that assessment focuses on reflection, not phrase perfection
- Result: Students took more creative risks

### What Students Said

**Positive feedback:**
- "I actually enjoyed this - it felt personal, not just homework"
- "Talking to ChatGPT made me think about my phrase in new ways"
- "My family loved the phrase I created - I might actually keep using it"

**Challenges noted:**
- "The in-class part was stressful at first because I didn't know what to do"
- "I wish we had more examples before starting"
- "It was weird asking ChatGPT about something I made"

### Future Refinements

**More scaffolding at the start:**
- Bring 3-5 strong examples to show in class
- Do a quick brainstorm activity first (2 minutes: list possible topics)
- Consider pair-share before solo invention

**Clearer assessment criteria:**
- Some students still worried about "getting it right"
- Show example reflections (with permission) of what depth looks like
- Emphasize: "I'm grading your thinking, not your phrase's popularity"

---

## Key Takeaways for Faculty

### Why This Redesign Succeeded

1. **Protected the creative core** - AI can't replicate personal/cultural invention done in class without devices

2. **Repositioned AI meaningfully** - From generator to conversation partner for reflection

3. **Assessment aligned with learning** - Grading reflection and critical thinking, not just the phrase itself

4. **Maintained authenticity** - Student work became personal again

5. **Leveraged diversity** - Multilingual students brought rich cultural-linguistic inventions

### Principles for Other Courses

**Ask yourself:**
- Where is AI replacing student thinking in my assignments?
- What's the core creative/personal work that needs protection?
- How can AI enhance reflection instead of replacing creation?
- What am I really trying to assess?

**Then redesign:**
- Move creative work in-class or structure it so AI can't do it
- Give AI a specific role (conversation, analysis, refinement)
- Assess reflection and critical thinking, not just final product
- Provide structure while allowing creativity

---

## Questions for Your Context

1. **What assignment has become generic?** Where are you seeing copy-paste AI responses?

2. **What's the irreplaceable creative work?** What requires personal insight, lived experience, or cultural knowledge?

3. **How could in-class time protect that work?** What could students create without AI access?

4. **How could AI support reflection?** What could students discuss with AI after creating?

5. **What would you assess?** How do you evaluate genuine learning vs. AI output?

cf:T4dd7,# Anthropology Word-Weaving: Full Simulation

> **Original Assignment Credit:** This assignment is adapted from Mike Wesch's Word-Weaving exercise.
> Original assignment: [https://anth101.com/challenge4/](https://anth101.com/challenge4/)
> The adaptation described here was developed at Dawson College to address challenges when AI tools became widely available.

---

## Assignment Context

**Course:** Introduction to Anthropology (381-1N1-DW)
**Assignment:** Word-Weaving (10% of course grade)
**Class size:** ~40 students
**Format:** Part 1 in-class (4%), Part 2 take-home (6%)

**Learning goals:**
- Understand how language shapes thought and culture
- Practice metalinguistic awareness (seeing your own seeing)
- Engage with concepts from course (metaphors we live by, linguistic relativity)
- Develop cultural observation skills

---

## The Problem: AI Killed the Original Assignment

### Original Format (All Take-Home)

**Assignment:**
1. Invent a new phrase or metaphor
2. Introduce it to family/friends
3. Reflect on the experience

**What students submitted before AI:**
- Creative, personal phrases
- Thoughtful reflections on reactions
- Cultural and linguistic diversity

**What students submitted after AI became widespread:**
- Generic AI-generated phrases like "time gardening" or "emotional weather"
- Identical examples across multiple students
- Superficial reflections clearly written by ChatGPT
- No personal investment or authentic learning

**The assignment became:** "Ask ChatGPT to invent a phrase, ask it to write a reflection, submit."

### Why the Redesign Was Necessary

**The learning objectives disappeared:**
- Students weren't practicing creativity
- No genuine cultural observation
- No metalinguistic awareness
- Just copying AI output

**The assessment became meaningless:**
- Couldn't distinguish student work from AI work
- Generic responses revealed no understanding
- Grading felt like checking if they ran the prompt correctly

---

## The Redesign: Protecting the Creative Core

### New Assignment Structure

**Part 1 (4%, in-class, ~40 minutes):**
- **Step 1:** Invent your phrase/metaphor (devices stowed)
- **Step 2:** Explain its meaning and intent

**Part 2 (6%, take-home, due 1 week later):**
- **Step 3:** Test phrase with family/friends + converse with ChatGPT
- **Step 4:** Reflect on both experiences

**Key change:** The creative and explanatory work happens in class where AI can't do it for them.

---

## Part 1: In-Class Session (40 minutes)

### Preparation (Before Class)

**Materials needed:**
- Printed assignment handout (students can't access devices)
- Examples from course readings (metaphors from Lakoff & Johnson)
- Examples of creative phrases (from previous years or your own)

**Room setup:**
- Clear instruction that all devices must be stowed
- Mention this is worth 4% and due at end of class

### In-Class Facilitation (Step by Step)

#### Introduction (5 minutes)

**Instructor explains:**

> "Today we're starting the Word-Weaving assignment. This is your chance to practice something anthropologists do: observe how language shapes our thinking and create new linguistic possibilities.
>
> You'll invent a new phrase or metaphor - something that doesn't exist yet but that you think would make the world better. Then you'll test it in the real world.
>
> The first part happens right now, in class, without devices. This is your creative work - it needs to be yours."

**Show examples from course:**
- "Argument is war" → What if we said "argument is dance"?
- "Time is money" → What if we said "time is water"?
- "Falling in love" → What could we say instead?

**Mention multilingual possibilities:**
> "Many of you speak multiple languages. You might invent a phrase in another language or bring an expression from your language that doesn't exist in English. That's fantastic - just make sure to explain it to me."

#### Step 1: Invention (15-20 minutes)

**Prompt on board:**

```
Invent a phrase or metaphor that you think would make the world a better place.

Think about:
- Arguments (what if they weren't wars?)
- Love (what if it wasn't falling?)
- Education (what if it wasn't filling containers?)
- Peace, the self, relationships, success, etc.

Your invention can be:
- A new metaphor (X is Y)
- A new phrase to describe feelings or experiences
- A translation/adaptation from another language
- A playful or serious expression

Remember:
- Must be your original invention
- Consider your audience (family/friends)
- Be respectful
```

**While students work:**
- Circulate and answer questions
- Encourage the hesitant ones ("Start with something small")
- Validate multilingual ideas
- Remind them they'll have time to refine later

**Common student concerns:**
- "What if it's not good?" → "That's fine, you'll test it and reflect"
- "Can I use [phrase in another language]?" → "Yes, as long as you explain it"
- "What if no one uses it?" → "That's data for your reflection!"

#### Step 2: Explanation (15-20 minutes)

**Prompt:**

```
In 1-2 paragraphs (5-7 sentences each), explain:

1. What does your phrase/metaphor mean?
2. What do you wish to communicate with it?
3. Who do you want to use it with?
4. What inspired you to create this?
5. What reception do you expect?
```

**Students write their explanations.**

**While students work:**
- Encourage specificity ("Who exactly will you try this with?")
- Ask about inspiration ("What made you think of this?")
- Check for clarity ("Could someone understand this?")

#### Collection (5 minutes)

**Collect both parts before students leave:**
- The invented phrase/metaphor
- The 1-2 paragraph explanation

**Give Part 2 instructions:**
- Handout with Steps 3-4 details
- Due date (typically 1 week later)
- Remind them they can now use ChatGPT as a conversation partner

---

## Part 2: Take-Home with AI Assist

### Step 3: Testing & Conversation

**Instructions for students:**

> **1. Test with humans:**
> Introduce your phrase or metaphor in conversation with family or friends as if it has always existed. See if they catch on and start using it themselves. If they ask about it, give them a strong pitch for why it should exist.
>
> **2. Converse with ChatGPT:**
> Ask ChatGPT how it understands your new expression. Have a conversation about the usage of your phrase/metaphor and whether it could be improved.

**Sample ChatGPT prompts (provided to students):**

**Prompt 1: Initial Understanding**
```
I created a new phrase/metaphor: "[your phrase here]"

The idea behind it is: [brief explanation]

How do you understand this expression? What do you think it communicates?
```

**Prompt 2: Usage Exploration**
```
Based on your understanding, in what situations or contexts do you think this phrase could be used?

Do you think it effectively communicates what I intended?
```

**Prompt 3: Refinement**
```
Do you have suggestions for how this phrase could be improved or made clearer?

What are the strengths and potential weaknesses of this expression?
```

**Prompt 4: Comparison**
```
When I introduced this to my [family/friends], they [describe their reaction].

How does your understanding and reaction compare to how humans responded to it?
```

### Step 4: Reflection

**Instructions for students:**

> Write 2 paragraphs (5-7 sentences each) describing:
>
> **Paragraph 1:** What happened when you introduced the expression to family/friends? How was it received? What did you observe about their reactions?
>
> **Paragraph 2:** What did ChatGPT think about your phrase? How did its response compare to human reactions? Is there anything you would choose to improve in your phrase or metaphor? Why or why not?

**Step 2 explanation (in-class):**
> "I grew up speaking Portuguese and English. In Portuguese, 'saudade' means a deep longing for something or someone absent - it's more than just missing someone. I created 'saudade-weaving' to describe how we build connections across distance through small acts - sending messages, sharing memories, keeping traditions alive across countries.
>
> I want to use this with my family who live in Brazil and Canada. I was inspired by how we stay connected despite the distance. I expect my family will understand because they know saudade, but my English-speaking friends might find it beautiful but hard to grasp."

**Step 3 human testing:**
> "I tried it with my mom during a video call. I said 'We're saudade-weaving right now' and she immediately smiled and understood. She said it perfectly captured what we do. My Canadian friend asked what it meant, and when I explained, she said she wished English had a word like that."

**Step 3 ChatGPT conversation (excerpt):**
> Student: "I created 'saudade-weaving' to describe building connections across distance. How do you understand it?"
>
> ChatGPT: "This is a beautiful hybrid expression. 'Saudade' carries that uniquely Portuguese sense of longing, while 'weaving' suggests active creation... it evokes the idea of maintaining bonds through intentional effort despite physical separation."

**Step 4 reflection:**
> "My family immediately understood saudade-weaving because they live it - we're constantly bridging distances. ChatGPT understood it intellectually and gave good suggestions, but my mom's reaction was emotional and real. She felt seen by the phrase.
>
> ChatGPT suggested I could make it clearer for English speakers by explaining saudade first. That's practical but kind of misses the point - some feelings don't translate neatly. I like keeping the Portuguese word because it honors that cultural specificity. I wouldn't change it."

**Why this works:**
- Personal cultural knowledge (saudade) that AI couldn't generate
- Authentic family connection and observation
- Critical reflection comparing AI's intellectual understanding vs. human emotional response
- Student owns their creative choice based on reflection

### Example 3: Phrase for Emotions

**Step 2 explanation (in-class):**
> "I created 'holding weather' to describe sitting with someone through their emotional storm without trying to fix it. We say 'weather the storm' for ourselves, but what about being present for someone else's storm? Holding weather means you're there, steady, not fixing or fleeing.

>

I'm keeping it as is because my friend's emotional response showed it works. The phrase isn't perfect linguistically, but it serves the purpose I intended - naming an experience that needed naming."

- AI provided linguistic analysis that student evaluated critically
- Student made reasoned choice to keep phrase despite AI's concerns

## Assessment Rubric

### Part 1: In-Class Creation (4%)

**Phrase/Metaphor Invention (2%)**
- ✅ Original creation (not AI-generated or widely known)
- ✅ Clear and understandable
- ✅ Thoughtfully conceived

**Explanation (2%)**
- ✅ Meaning clearly explained
- ✅ Audience and purpose identified
- ✅ Personal inspiration or reasoning shared
- ✅ Expected reception considered

**Note:** Part 1 is primarily assessed for completion and personal engagement, not perfection.

### Part 2: Take-Home Reflection (6%)

**Testing & ChatGPT Conversation (2%)**
- ✅ Evidence of real-world testing with humans
- ✅ Documented conversation with ChatGPT
- ✅ Multiple aspects of the phrase explored with AI

**Reflection Quality (4%)**
- **Depth:** Goes beyond surface-level description to analyze reactions
- **Comparison:** Meaningfully compares human and AI responses
- **Critical thinking:** Evaluates feedback and makes reasoned choices
- **Personal insight:** Shows metalinguistic awareness and cultural observation

**What we're looking for:**
- Not just "my friend liked it" but "why did they react that way?"
- Not just "ChatGPT suggested X" but "do I agree? Why or why not?"
- Evidence of genuine reflection on language, culture, and meaning
- Personal voice and engagement throughout

**Red flags (possible AI-written reflection):**
- Generic observations ("the phrase was well-received")
- No specific quotes or details from human testing
- Overly polished academic language without personal voice
- No critical evaluation of ChatGPT's suggestions

## Iteration Notes (From Instructor)

### What Worked Immediately

**In-class creation:**
- Students produced authentic, creative work
- Multilingual inventions emerged naturally
- Personal investment was visible

**AI as conversation partner:**
- Students used ChatGPT thoughtfully for analysis
- Comparisons between human and AI reactions were insightful
- Reflections showed genuine engagement

### Adjustments Made

**Providing more structure:**
- Initially students felt lost with open-ended creativity
- Added more examples from course readings
- Provided clearer constraints (categories, audience guidance)
- Result: Students engaged more confidently

**Sample ChatGPT prompts:**
- Some students didn't know how to start the AI conversation
- Provided sample prompts to scaffold the interaction
- Students still personalized them based on their phrases

**Emphasizing reflection over product:**
- Some students worried their phrases weren't "good enough"
- Clarified that assessment focuses on reflection, not phrase perfection
- Result: Students took more creative risks

### What Students Said

**Positive feedback:**
- "I actually enjoyed this - it felt personal, not just homework"
- "Talking to ChatGPT made me think about my phrase in new ways"
- "My family loved the phrase I created - I might actually keep using it"

**Challenges noted:**
- "The in-class part was stressful at first because I didn't know what to do"
- "I wish we had more examples before starting"
- "It was weird asking ChatGPT about something I made"

### Future Refinements

**More scaffolding at the start:**
- Bring 3-5 strong examples to show in class
- Do a quick brainstorm activity first (2 minutes: list possible topics)
- Consider pair-share before solo invention

**Clearer assessment criteria:**
- Some students still worried about "getting it right"
- Show example reflections (with permission) of what depth looks like
- Emphasize: "I'm grading your thinking, not your phrase's popularity"

## Key Takeaways for Faculty

### Why This Redesign Succeeded

1. **Protected the creative core** - AI can't replicate personal/cultural invention done in class without devices

2. **Repositioned AI meaningfully** - From generator to conversation partner for reflection

3. **Assessment aligned with learning** - Grading reflection and critical thinking, not just the phrase itself

4. **Maintained authenticity** - Student work became personal again

5. **Leveraged diversity** - Multilingual students brought rich cultural-linguistic inventions

### Principles for Other Courses

**Ask yourself:**
- Where is AI replacing student thinking in my assignments?
- What's the core creative/personal work that needs protection?
- How can AI enhance reflection instead of replacing creation?
- What am I really trying to assess?

**Then redesign:**
- Move creative work in-class or structure it so AI can't do it
- Give AI a specific role (conversation, analysis, refinement)
- Assess reflection and critical thinking, not just final product
- Provide structure while allowing creativity
