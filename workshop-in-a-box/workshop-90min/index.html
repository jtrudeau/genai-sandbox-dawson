Workshop-in-a-Box: 90-Minute Faculty Sandbox

Welcome to the GenAI Sandbox.

This is not a training session about AI tools. This is an invitation to experiment—to try small things in your teaching, notice what happens, and decide what serves your students’ learning.

Over the next 90 minutes, you’ll:

  1. Understand what the sandbox approach is (and isn’t)
  2. Experience using AI as a thoughtful teaching tool (you’ll be in the student role)
  3. Brainstorm experiments you can try in your own teaching
  4. Connect with colleagues exploring similar questions
  5. Leave with ideas and next steps

Part 1: What Is the Sandbox? (20 minutes)

The Problem

Many of us feel caught between two forces:

Pressure to adopt AI quickly - “Transform your teaching! Increase efficiency! Students are already using it!”

Pressure to resist AI entirely - “It’s plagiarism! It will ruin learning! We can’t trust it!”

Neither extreme serves our students well.

The Sandbox Approach

A sandbox is a space where you can try things without high stakes. In teaching, it means:

Small experiments - Not overhauling your whole course, just trying one thing Reversible - You can stop anytime if it’s not working Documented - You write down what actually happened, not what you hoped would happen Reflective - The learning is in noticing what emerges, not just “did it work?”

Guiding Principles

Humanism - Students are humans learning and growing, not efficiency problems to solve

Responsibility - We acknowledge risks, consider consequences, and act with care

Transparency - We’re honest with students about what we’re trying and why

These aren’t abstract ideals. They’re practical guardrails for thoughtful experimentation.

What Makes a Good Sandbox Experiment?

Example: Testing AI-Generated Discussion Prompts

The experiment: “I teach sociology. My discussion prompts feel repetitive. I’ll use AI to generate 5 alternative prompts for next week’s class on social stratification. I’ll compare them to my usual prompts, select the best 3 (AI or mine), and observe discussion quality in class.”

Why this is a good sandbox experiment:

  • ✅ Small - Just one class session, just discussion prompts
  • ✅ Specific - Clear what I’m testing (prompt variety and discussion quality)
  • ✅ Reversible - If it doesn’t work, I go back to my usual approach next week
  • ✅ Documented - I’ll save screenshots of prompts and take notes during class
  • ✅ Transparent - I’ll tell students I’m experimenting with AI-assisted prompt design

What I’ll learn:

  • Can AI help me break out of prompt patterns?
  • Do students engage differently with AI-generated vs. my usual prompts?
  • Is this worth my time to continue?

Not this:

❌ “I’m going to use AI for everything and see what happens” ❌ “I’ll have AI grade all my essays from now on” ❌ “Students can use AI however they want and I won’t interfere”

Demonstration: Seeing It In Action

Your facilitator will now demonstrate a sandbox experiment using one of the workshop prompts. Pay attention to:

  • What makes the prompt effective?
  • What does the AI output include (and what does it miss)?
  • How would you evaluate whether this is useful?
  • What would you need to document if you tried this?

[Your facilitator will show output from one of the Set 1 prompts and discuss it with the group]

Transition: Now You Try

You’ve just seen an example. Now you’ll experience what it feels like to use AI as a brainstorming tool for your own teaching.

Part 2: Discovery & Sharing (50 minutes)

Activity 1: Hands-On Brainstorming (10-15 minutes)

Everyone will do this together.

You’re about to use AI to brainstorm sandbox experiments for YOUR teaching. This puts you in the position your students would be in—using AI as a structured tool for thinking, not as a shortcut.

What to do:

  1. Open the workshop prompts document → Workshop Prompts

  2. Go to Set 2: Participant Hands-On Prompt

  3. Fill in your context:

    • Your discipline or subject area
    • Your course level (intro? advanced? mixed?)
    • One specific challenge in your teaching
    • What you value most in teaching

  4. Copy the complete prompt into ChatGPT (or another LLM if you prefer)

  5. Review the 5 experiment ideas the AI generates

  6. Pick ONE idea that feels most relevant to you

  7. Take notes:

    • Which idea resonated? Why?
    • What surprised you about the AI’s suggestions?
    • What would you need to modify to make this workable?
    • What concerns or questions does this raise?

You have 10-15 minutes. Don’t overthink it—this is exploratory.

Small Group Sharing (5-7 minutes)

Find 2-3 people near you (ideally from different disciplines).

Each person shares (90 seconds each):

  • What experiment idea you picked
  • Why it resonated
  • One question or concern you have

As listeners:

  • What sounds promising about their idea?
  • What would you be curious to know if they tried it?

Activity 2: Go Deeper (15-20 minutes)

Now that you’ve explored broadly, let’s go deeper into one specific area.

What to do:

  1. Go back to the workshop prompts document → Workshop Prompts

  2. Go to Set 3: Participant Brainstorming Prompts

  3. Choose the scenario that best matches what you want to explore:

    • Scenario A: Generating teaching materials (prompts, problems, examples)
    • Scenario B: Providing feedback and assessment support
    • Scenario C: Scaffolding for struggling students
    • Scenario D: Brainstorming assignment redesign
    • Scenario E: Student-facing AI use (teaching critical evaluation)

  4. Fill in your context and copy the prompt into ChatGPT

  5. Review what the AI generates - this should be more detailed and actionable than the first activity

  6. Take notes:

    • What parts of this feel immediately usable?
    • What would you need to adapt or change?
    • What risks or ethical considerations come to mind?
    • What would you need to learn more about before trying this?

You have 15-20 minutes.

Group Sharing by Scenario (10-15 minutes)

Form groups based on which scenario you chose.

  • All “Scenario A” people together
  • All “Scenario B” people together
  • And so on…

In your scenario group, discuss:

  1. What ideas emerged across different disciplines?
  2. Common challenges or concerns - what came up for multiple people?
  3. What would make this experiment actually doable in your context?
  4. What support or resources would help you try this?

Each group will share ONE key insight with the whole room in a moment.

Whole Group Insights (10 minutes)

Each scenario group shares:

  • One promising idea
  • One challenge that emerged
  • One question you’re still sitting with

Part 3: Next Steps & Resources (20 minutes)

What You’ve Done Today

You’ve:

  • ✅ Experienced using AI as a thoughtful tool (not a shortcut)
  • ✅ Generated multiple experiment ideas for your own teaching
  • ✅ Heard how colleagues across disciplines are thinking about this
  • ✅ Identified specific areas to explore further

Most importantly: You’ve practiced the sandbox mindset—curiosity, small experiments, honest observation.

What To Do Next

This week (Reflection):

  1. Review your notes from today’s activities
  2. Pick ONE experiment that feels doable—not perfect, just doable
  3. Make it smaller if needed - what’s the smallest version you could try?

Next 2-4 weeks (Action):

  1. Design your experiment using the sandbox template:

    • What am I trying to learn?
    • What will I actually do?
    • What evidence will I collect?
    • How will I tell students about this?

    👉 Find the template here: Faculty Resources - Experiment Template

  2. Try it - run your experiment in one class, one assignment, one small context

  3. Document what happens - honestly, not optimistically

    • What surprised you?
    • What worked or didn’t work?
    • What would you change next time?

End-of-semester (Sharing):

  1. Reconnect with this cohort (optional but encouraged)
  2. Share what you tried - successes, failures, and in-betweens
  3. Decide what to continue, modify, or stop

Resources to Support You

For designing experiments:

  • Faculty Experiment Template - Structured guide for planning and documenting experiments
  • Example Use Cases - Real experiments tried by faculty across disciplines

For understanding AI tools:

  • Tool Comparison Guide - When to use what (ChatGPT, Claude, etc.)
  • Effective Prompting Basics - How to ask AI for what you actually need

For addressing concerns:

  • Academic Integrity & Transparency - How to set expectations with students
  • Equity Considerations - Ensuring fair access and avoiding bias
  • When AI Falls Short - Knowing what NOT to use AI for

For facilitators:

  • Facilitator Guide - How to run and adapt this workshop

Building a Community of Practice

You don’t have to do this alone.

Option 1: Stay connected with today’s cohort

  • Optional check-in in 4-6 weeks
  • Share experiments, troubleshoot challenges
  • [Sign up for cohort updates] (facilitator provides link/mechanism)

Option 2: Find a partner

  • Pair up with someone from today
  • Check in monthly, share what you’re trying
  • Provide feedback and encouragement

Option 3: Department-level exploration

  • Bring this approach back to your department
  • Run experiments in parallel, share findings
  • Build discipline-specific best practices

The goal: Build judgment together about what serves learning.

Three Invitations

As you leave today, consider these three invitations:

1. Permission to start small

You don’t need to have it all figured out. One small experiment. One class session. That’s enough.

2. Permission to fail

If your experiment doesn’t work, that’s not failure—that’s data. Document what you learned and try something different.

3. Permission to say no

Not every experiment will be worth continuing. Not every AI tool will serve your teaching. You get to decide based on what you observe, not what others say you should do.

Final Reflection (5 minutes)

Take a moment to write:

One thing I’m taking away from today:

[Your reflection]

One experiment I’m committing to try:

[Your commitment - be specific]

One thing I’m still uncertain about:

[Your question or concern]

Optional: Share one of these with the person next to you.

Closing Thoughts

This is not about becoming an AI expert.

This is about staying curious about your teaching, trying small things, and noticing what happens.

AI in education is emergent, contested, and context-dependent. There is no single right way to use it—or not use it.

What we have is:

  • A method for navigating uncertainty (sandbox experimentation)
  • Each other (peer community and shared learning)
  • A commitment to put student learning first (not efficiency, not novelty)

The sandbox is always open. Try something. Notice what happens. Share what you learn.

Thank you for your curiosity, your questions, and your commitment to thoughtful teaching.

Workshop complete. Next step: Try one small thing.
