# Run a Workshop

Use this page to run a standalone sandbox workshop.
Use it as the main guide for a 90-minute or longer session.

A 90-minute format is a good starting point because it gives facilitators enough time to do five things well:

- frame the sandbox
- show why context matters
- add enough context before using AI
- work through one small example
- leave with a concrete next step

## Suggested Pacing

| Duration | Recommended use | Practical note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 30-45 minutes | first meeting focused only on experiment discovery | use the short-session guidance in [Facilitator Home](/facilitator) instead of compressing the full workshop |
| 75 minutes | short PD slot or experienced group | keep the same structure and reduce scenario depth |
| 90 minutes | first implementation or varied participant backgrounds | best balance for complete outcomes |
| 120+ minutes | department team or deeper planning | add more time for adaptation and reflection |

## Session Outcomes

Aim for participants to leave with:

- a specific task or artifact they want to work on
- a brief or prompt they can build on
- a small example they could realistically try
- a sense of what they want the result to do
- a sense of what they would want to review, adapt, or avoid
- one possible next step if they want to continue

## Suggested Agenda (90 Minutes)

- Part 1 (20 min): frame the sandbox and compare a vague ask with the same ask plus context
- Part 2 (45 min): write a short context and work through one small example
- Part 3 (25 min): pause, review what happened, and look ahead

## Before You Run the Session

Decide these basics before you begin:

- one demo you will show, including the vague version and the version with context
- one scenario path you are most likely to use from [Workshop Scenarios](/facilitator/workshop-scenarios)
- how people will work in the room and what AI access they will have
- one simple starter participants can adapt, such as the quick example, a short context template, or Set 2 from [Workshop Prompts](/workshop-in-a-box/workshop-prompts)
- what boundaries, cautions, or follow-up options you want to mention during the session

## Part 1: Show Why Context Matters

### Frame the Session

Open with purpose:

- this is not tool training
- this is not a survey of everything AI can do
- any chat-style AI tool can work for the workshop
- we will work from one specific task in a course, support, or other work context
- we will start with something small enough to try and learn from
- the goal is to leave with a starting point they can use for one task

Introduce the simple model:

- LLMs are good at filling in patterns
- if your ask is vague, they fill in the gaps with generic assumptions
- if you give a specific task, enough background, what you want the result to do, and any limits, the response is easier to judge
- the educator still sets the standard and decides what to keep, revise, or reject

In this workshop, **context** means the details of your teaching or support situation that help you judge whether the response fits your situation.

- the prompt is what you type
- the context is the situation you bring with it

The core lesson is simple: set the context before you ask.

  <div className="mb-3">
    <strong>With context:</strong> &quot;I teach Intro Biology. I need 5 short review questions for next week&apos;s class on cell transport. My students often confuse diffusion, osmosis, and active transport. I want questions that reveal those misconceptions without being too difficult. Keep the language clear enough for first-year students. I will check scientific accuracy and whether the questions match what we covered in class.&quot;
  </div>
  <div className="text-sm text-gray-600 dark:text-gray-400">
    The prompt is the request for quiz questions. The context is the course, timing, student difficulty, desired level, and what the educator will review.
  </div>
</Card>

### Demonstrate a Vague Ask and the Same Ask with Context

Use one facilitator prompt from [Workshop Prompts](/workshop-in-a-box/workshop-prompts), but do not begin with the full version.
Start with a vague ask first, then improve it in front of the group.

Suggested sequence:

1. show a short request tied to a teaching or support task
2. ask the group what is missing
3. add the task, the course or support-work context, what you want the result to do, any limits, and what you plan to check
4. run the version with context
5. compare the two outputs briefly

Debrief with:

- what changed once the context became more specific?
- what still needed human review?
- what would make this worth using in your practice?

Keep the demonstration short.
The point is to make the contrast visible, not to impress people with the output.

## Part 2: Work Through One Small Example

<Card className="border-l-4 border-l-emerald-500 p-6 my-8">
  <div className="mb-3 font-medium">
    Quick example: from short notes to a fuller prompt
  </div>
  <div className="mb-3">
    A participant might begin with five short notes:
    <ul className="list-disc ml-6 mt-2">
      <li><strong>Task:</strong> write 3 discussion questions</li>
      <li><strong>Context:</strong> Intro Sociology, next week&apos;s class on social stratification</li>
      <li><strong>What I want the result to do:</strong> invite interpretation, not just definitions</li>
      <li><strong>Limits:</strong> workable in 10 minutes, clear enough for first-year students</li>
      <li><strong>Check:</strong> do the questions fit the reading and open stronger discussion?</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div className="mb-3">
    Those notes can then become a fuller prompt or brief for AI.
  </div>
  <div className="text-sm text-gray-600 dark:text-gray-400">
    The prepared prompts on the prompts page already include this fuller kind of context on purpose. If you use one, say that out loud: the extra detail is part of what makes the result easier to judge.
  </div>
</Card>

### Activity A: Choose One Task and Note the Context (10-12 minutes)

Tell participants they are now going to work on one task of their own.
They do not need a finished prompt yet.
They only need enough notes to make the task clear.

This can be done on paper, in a shared document, in the [interactive planner](/user/run-experiment#interactive-planner), or in the AI tool itself if people prefer to draft there first.

If people are working in pairs or small groups, ask each group to choose one task together.

Ask participants to note five things:

- the task they want help with
- the course, support, or other work situation
- what they want the result to help them do
- any limit, caution, or instruction the AI should respect
- what they will want to check themselves before using anything

Tell them to work in notes, not full sentences.
If they already have a draft, assignment, instructions, resource, or support notes open, they can work from that instead of starting from a blank page.

As you circulate, help people narrow to one task.
If someone lists several goals, ask:

- what would be most useful to work on first?
- what is the first thing you want the AI to help you with?
- what would you want to look at and say, "yes, this is worth revising"?

If participants get stuck, offer a quick way in:

- borrow the quick example above and replace the details with their own
- open Set 2 in [Workshop Prompts](/workshop-in-a-box/workshop-prompts) and fill in the five fields instead of starting from a blank page
- choose a prepared scenario that is close enough and personalize it later

By the end of this activity, each person or group should have one task and a few notes they can turn into a fuller prompt or brief in Activity B.

### Activity B: Turn It Into a More Detailed Prompt or Brief (12-15 minutes)

Use Set 2 from [Workshop Prompts](/workshop-in-a-box/workshop-prompts).

Explain that the prepared prompts are more detailed on purpose.
They show what changes when the task, context, quality bar, and limits are stated clearly.

Invite participants to turn that short context into one prompt or brief they can actually test.

Ask them to notice:

- what became clearer once they gave more context
- what still came back too generic
- what they would need to review before using anything

### Optional Pair or Small-Group Exchange (8-10 minutes)

Use this only if it fits the room setup.

- if people have their own devices, use pairs or groups of 2-3 after the individual activity
- if people are already sharing a device or thread, keep them in those working groups
- if access is mixed or limited, skip this and do a short whole-group debrief instead

If you use this step, ask participants to share:

- the task they chose
- one part of the context that changed the result most
- one limit or check they want to keep

### Choose the Closest Scenario (10-12 minutes)

Ask participants to choose the one Set 3 scenario that best matches the task they already started.
They are not starting over.
They are choosing the closest prompt shape for the same piece of work.

Keep this moving:

- ask what the task is mostly about
- match it to the closest scenario
- if two scenarios seem possible, pick one and continue

<Card className="border-l-4 border-l-violet-500 p-6 my-8">
  <div className="mb-3 font-medium">
    Quick examples
  </div>
  <ul className="list-disc ml-6">
    <li>if someone is working on discussion questions for Intro Sociology, Scenario D may be the best fit because the task is about discussion and participation</li>
    <li>if someone is working on practice problems or reading questions, Scenario A may be the closer fit because the task is mainly about teaching materials</li>
  </ul>
</Card>

The goal is not to browse the full page.
The goal is to deepen the one task they already started.

If time is tighter than expected, move people to [Rapid Start](/workshop-in-a-box/workshop-prompts#set-3-rapid-start) instead of rushing the full scenario prompts.

## Part 3: Pause, Review, and Look Ahead

### Use a Light-Weight Wrap-Up

Keep the close brief and focused.
By this point, participants have already done the main thinking.
Use this final part of the session to help them notice what seems worth keeping, what still needs review, and whether anything is worth continuing.

Choose the wrap-up format that fits the room:

- if people have their own device or shared thread, ask them to stay in the same conversation and use a short AI-assisted wrap-up
- if access or time is limited, do this as a spoken reflection and try to capture a few notes

If you are not using devices, ask the same questions out loud and invite people to jot notes.

### Close with Shared Takeaways

Bring the room back together in a way that fits the setting.
This can be a short spoken round, a few volunteers, a shared board, or a quick whole-group debrief.

Useful closing prompts:

- what changed when more context was added?
- what felt clearer or more worth keeping?
- what still needs human judgment?
- what would you want to try, adjust, or leave aside after this?

Participants do not need to answer all four.
Use one or two if time is short.

### End with a Simple Invitation

Close by summarizing what the group tried and what the optional next move could be.
Help people leave with a sense of completion and a possible path forward.

You might say:

- today was about seeing what changes when a vague ask becomes a more specific AI request
- if you want to keep developing the example you started, go to [Run an Experiment](/user/run-experiment#from-discovery-to-first-run)
- if you want to browse the sandbox more broadly first, start at [Welcome](/user) or the [Interactive Planner](/user/run-experiment#interactive-planner)

Choose one or two of these options based on the group.
Do not try to walk everyone through every follow-up link in the final minutes.

If you want more structured feedback, send a short survey after the workshop rather than using the close for that.
