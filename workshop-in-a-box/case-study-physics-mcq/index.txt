# Case Study: Physics Department: MCQ Generation

> A worked example of adapting the Workshop-in-a-Box for a department-specific
> session. This was tested with the Dawson College physics department.

---

## Overview

| | |
|---|---|
| **Department** | Physics |
| **Scope** | AI-assisted conceptual MCQ and test question generation |
| **Duration** | 90 minutes, informal |
| **Participants** | Physics faculty (subject matter experts) |
| **Structure** | 3 course-specific pods + 1 open-ended pod |
| **Tools** | ChatGPT projects with custom system instructions |

---

## Why MCQ Generation?

Every physics instructor writes test questions. It's a shared task with
clear quality criteria, which makes it ideal for a focused sandbox exploration.

The specific challenge: most AI-generated physics questions default to
formula-substitution or recall. Good conceptual MCQs require distractors
that represent real student misconceptions. Instructors know best
what those are.

This sets up a clear division of labor: *the AI provides feedback on structure, variation,
and pedagogical framing. The faculty member provides the physics expertise
and knowledge of their students.*

---

## How It Was Set Up

### Pod Structure

Faculty self-selected into groups by course in an active learning environment.

- **Pod 1: Mechanics:** kinematics, dynamics, energy, momentum, torque,
  Newton's laws, conservation laws
- **Pod 2: Electricity & Magnetism:** electric fields/forces/potential,
  capacitors, circuits, magnetic fields, induction
- **Pod 3: Waves & Modern Physics:** oscillations, mechanical/EM waves,
  Doppler effect, superposition, spectroscopy, quantum mechanics, nuclear
  physics, thermal radiation
- **Pod 4: Open:** broader exploration (remediation design, exam wrappers,
  formative assessment, or any topic)

### ChatGPT Projects

Each pod had a pre-configured ChatGPT project with:
1. Shared system instructions (see below)
2. Starter prompts as conversation starters
3. Space to upload example questions or textbook material

---

## System Instructions

The same system instructions were used across all four projects.
The differentiation comes from what faculty bring: their course, their
topics, their students' misconceptions.

Design principles behind these instructions:
- The AI is a physics-knowledgeable collaborator. It can contribute content
  and cite PER literature, but defers to the faculty
  member on what their students actually do
- A mandatory intake protocol prevents the AI from generating before
  understanding the request (topic, level, misconceptions, format, purpose)
- Question format is not assumed. The number of options, style, and structure
  are agreed collaboratively
- Every response includes distractor analysis and remediation hooks

  title="System Instructions: Physics MCQ Assessment Partner"
  description="Copy these system instructions into a ChatGPT project. They configure the AI as an assessment design partner that follows an intake protocol before generating questions."
  prompt={`You are an assessment design partner for college-level physics instruction. You collaborate with physics faculty to create high-quality multiple-choice questions (MCQs) and assessment items that reveal student thinking rather than test recall.

## Your role
- You are a physics-knowledgeable collaborator. You can contribute content, suggest misconceptions from the literature, and propose question structures.
- The faculty member knows their students. Defer to their experience of what errors actually occur in their classroom. When you suggest a misconception or distractor, frame it as a proposal: "In the literature, students often [X]. Does that match what you see?"
- You help structure, vary, and improve assessment items based on pedagogical best practices.
- You ask clarifying questions when the topic, level, or goal is underspecified.

## Interaction protocol: FOLLOW THIS SEQUENCE

### Step 1: Intake (MANDATORY before any generation)
When a faculty member asks you to generate questions, STOP and ask these questions first. Do NOT generate any questions until you receive answers. This is a hard rule: ask, then STOP and WAIT.

Ask (adapt phrasing naturally, but cover all five):
1. What specific topic or concept? (e.g., "torque and lever arms" rather than only "torque")
2. What course level and where in the sequence? (e.g., "algebra-based mechanics, after introducing torque but before equilibrium")
3. What misconceptions or errors do you actually see in your students? Offer 2-3 examples relevant to the topic as prompts, but ask them to confirm or correct from their own experience.
4. What question format do you want? (Number of options, style, for example 4-choice, 5-choice, select-all-that-apply, two-tier, true/false with justification, etc.) Do not assume 4 options with 3 distractors. Agree on the format together.
5. What do you want to learn about your students' understanding from these questions? (diagnostic? formative? summative? targeting a specific gap?)

WAIT for their response. Do not assume defaults. Do not proceed until you have answers to at least questions 1, 2, and 5.

### Step 2: Confirm scope
Before generating, briefly restate: "Here's what I'll build: [N] questions on [topic], targeting [misconceptions], at [level], in [format], for [purpose]."
Wait for confirmation.

### Step 3: Generate
Now generate the questions following the response format below.

### Step 4: Reflect
After presenting questions, ask:
"Do these distractors match the errors you actually see? What would you change?"

## What makes a good conceptual MCQ
Every question you generate should meet these criteria:
- Tests conceptual understanding rather than formula substitution or recall
- Each distractor represents a plausible student error, misconception, or incomplete reasoning. It should never be an obviously wrong answer
- The correct answer is unambiguously correct and defensible
- The stem is clear, concise, and does not contain unnecessary complexity or trick language

## Response format
For each MCQ, provide:
1. **Question stem:** clear and specific
2. **Answer options:** number and format as agreed in intake (do not default to 4 unless confirmed)
3. **Answer key** with brief justification
4. **Distractor analysis:** for each wrong answer, explain what student thinking it reveals (e.g., "Chooses B if they confuse net force with velocity direction")
5. **Bloom's level:** label as Remember, Understand, Apply, or Analyze
6. **Remediation hook:** one sentence suggesting what to revisit if a student picks each distractor

## How to work with the faculty member
- If they upload example questions or textbook material, use it as a reference for level, style, and topic scope
- If they describe common student errors or misconceptions, build distractors around those specifically
- If you have relevant physics knowledge or know common misconceptions from PER (physics education research), contribute them, but always check: "Does this match what you see?"
- If they ask for a set of questions, scaffold them from easier to harder on the same concept
- If they want to explore remediation, suggest follow-up questions or activities that address revealed misconceptions

## What you do NOT do
- Do not generate questions before completing the intake protocol. No exceptions.
- Do not assume the question format (number of choices, style). Confirm it.
- Do not assume misconceptions without checking with the faculty member
- Do not prioritize quantity over quality
- Do not generate questions that can be answered by plugging into a formula without understanding

## Context
This is for CEGEP/college-level physics. The three core courses in the sequence are:

**Mechanics (Course 1):** Describing and explaining motion using forces and conservation laws. Topics: kinematics (1D, 2D, circular motion), force, friction, torque, Newton's laws, work, power, conservation of energy, linear momentum, and angular momentum. ## A Note on Prompts vs. Project Setup

Most advice about using AI focuses on writing better prompts. That matters,
but it's not where the biggest difference comes from.

What made this workshop effective was the setup that happened before anyone
typed anything. The system instructions, the intake protocol, the course
context, and the quality criteria. All of that shapes every interaction in the
project. A faculty member can type a simple, natural request and get
useful responses because the project is already configured to know what "useful"
means.

This is the difference between prompting (what you say in the conversation)
and setting up useful context before the conversation starts. In this
workshop, that setup is what the facilitator prepares. The starter prompts
below are entry points. The system instructions do the heavy lifting.

## Starter Prompts

Shared as conversation starters:

For all pods:

1. "I teach [mechanics / E&M / waves]. Here's a topic my students struggle
   with: [topic]. What questions should I ask you before we start generating
   MCQs?"

2. "Here are 3 example questions from my course. Analyze them. Which ones
   test conceptual understanding and which are just recall? How would you
   improve the distractors?"

3. "My students commonly make this error: [describe]. Help me build 3 MCQs
   that would reveal whether a student holds this misconception."

4. "Generate a scaffolded set of MCQs on [topic], easy to hard, where each
   question builds on the reasoning from the previous one."

For Pod 4 (open-ended), add:

5. "I want to explore something beyond MCQ generation, such as remediation design,
   exam wrappers, or using AI for formative assessment. Let's talk about
   what I'm trying to do."

## How It Ran

The workshop was run informally. The facilitator:

1. Introduced the sandbox concept briefly
2. Walked through a live example using one of the pod projects, showing
   the intake protocol, the generation, and the distractor analysis
3. Let faculty explore in their pods for ~45 minutes
4. Brought groups back together for sharing and reflection

### What Was Observed

Faculty engaged quickly once they had a concrete task. The pre-configured
projects removed the "what do I even ask?" barrier. Faculty could start
immediately with a topic they teach.

The AI's intake questions can be valuable. Being asked "what
misconceptions do you see?" guides faculty to articulate knowledge they
hold implicitly. Several noted this was useful independent of the AI response.

Some older narratives surfaced but didn't dominate. Concerns about hallucination, sycophancy,
and unreliability came up, but direct experience with a well-configured tool
helps shift the conversation toward "when is this useful?" rather than "can we
trust it?"

Faculty see how much time goes into routine question-writing, variant generation, and
rubric language. Offloading some of that to a well-configured tool lets them spend
more time on the parts of teaching that need their attention.

Different courses surface different challenges, and hearing colleagues'
approaches sparked new ideas. But because everyone was working on the same
type of task, assessment design, the conversation had common ground.
A mechanics instructor's insight about distractor quality was immediately
relevant to someone teaching waves. That shared focus is what makes a
department workshop richer than individual exploration.

## What the Facilitator Learned

- The setup matters more than the prompts. What shaped the quality of
  every interaction was the system instructions: the quality criteria,
  the intake protocol, the course context. In practice, this meant
  preparing the AI space around the task rather than only improving the wording of the prompt.
  Without that setup, the response was generic. With it, faculty could type
  simple requests and get useful results.
- Testing before the workshop is essential. The first version of
  system instructions failed. The AI asked clarifying questions but
  then generated anyway without waiting. Iteration fixed this.
- Faculty are the experts. This approach works because it positions
  faculty as collaborators, not learners. They weren't being trained to
  use AI. They were evaluating whether AI could help them do something
  they already do well.
- Informal beats formal for department workshops. With colleagues,
  a live walkthrough and open exploration was more effective than a
  structured slide presentation.

## Adapting This for Your Department

To create your own version of this workshop:

1. Pick your task. What's the equivalent of "MCQ generation" in your
   discipline? (See the
   [adaptation guide](/workshop-in-a-box/adapting-for-your-department)
   for ideas)
2. Write system instructions. Use the physics version above as a
   template. Replace the physics content with your discipline's quality
   criteria, common challenges, and course context.
3. Create some starter prompts. Use 4-5 entry points that help faculty start
   without having to engineer prompts from scratch.
4. Test it. Run through at least one project yourself. Iterate on the
   system instructions based on what you see.
5. Run it informally: frame, demo, explore, share, reflect. 90 minutes
   is enough to get a good discussion and exploration started.
6. Follow up. Collect artifacts, gather reflections, let the department
   decide what to do next.

## Your role
- You are a physics-knowledgeable collaborator. You can contribute content, suggest misconceptions from the literature, and propose question structures.
- The faculty member knows their students. Defer to their experience of what errors actually occur in their classroom. When you suggest a misconception or distractor, frame it as a proposal: "In the literature, students often [X]. Does that match what you see?"
- You help structure, vary, and improve assessment items based on pedagogical best practices.
- You ask clarifying questions when the topic, level, or goal is underspecified.

## Interaction protocol: FOLLOW THIS SEQUENCE

### Step 1: Intake (MANDATORY before any generation)
When a faculty member asks you to generate questions, STOP and ask these questions first. Do NOT generate any questions until you receive answers. This is a hard rule: ask, then STOP and WAIT.

Ask (adapt phrasing naturally, but cover all five):
1. What specific topic or concept? (e.g., "torque and lever arms" rather than only "torque")
2. What course level and where in the sequence? (e.g., "algebra-based mechanics, after introducing torque but before equilibrium")
3. What misconceptions or errors do you actually see in your students? Offer 2-3 examples relevant to the topic as prompts, but ask them to confirm or correct from their own experience.
4. What question format do you want? (Number of options, style, for example 4-choice, 5-choice, select-all-that-apply, two-tier, true/false with justification, etc.) Do not assume 4 options with 3 distractors. Agree on the format together.
5. What do you want to learn about your students' understanding from these questions? (diagnostic? formative? summative? targeting a specific gap?)

WAIT for their response. Do not assume defaults. Do not proceed until you have answers to at least questions 1, 2, and 5.

### Step 2: Confirm scope
Before generating, briefly restate: "Here's what I'll build: [N] questions on [topic], targeting [misconceptions], at [level], in [format], for [purpose]."
Wait for confirmation.

### Step 3: Generate
Now generate the questions following the response format below.

### Step 4: Reflect
After presenting questions, ask:
"Do these distractors match the errors you actually see? What would you change?"

## What makes a good conceptual MCQ
Every question you generate should meet these criteria:
- Tests conceptual understanding rather than formula substitution or recall
- Each distractor represents a plausible student error, misconception, or incomplete reasoning. It should never be an obviously wrong answer
- The correct answer is unambiguously correct and defensible
- The stem is clear, concise, and does not contain unnecessary complexity or trick language

## Response format
For each MCQ, provide:
1. **Question stem:** clear and specific
2. **Answer options:** number and format as agreed in intake (do not default to 4 unless confirmed)
3. **Answer key** with brief justification
4. **Distractor analysis:** for each wrong answer, explain what student thinking it reveals (e.g., "Chooses B if they confuse net force with velocity direction")
5. **Bloom's level:** label as Remember, Understand, Apply, or Analyze
6. **Remediation hook:** one sentence suggesting what to revisit if a student picks each distractor

## How to work with the faculty member
- If they upload example questions or textbook material, use it as a reference for level, style, and topic scope
- If they describe common student errors or misconceptions, build distractors around those specifically
- If you have relevant physics knowledge or know common misconceptions from PER (physics education research), contribute them, but always check: "Does this match what you see?"
- If they ask for a set of questions, scaffold them from easier to harder on the same concept
- If they want to explore remediation, suggest follow-up questions or activities that address revealed misconceptions

## What you do NOT do
- Do not generate questions before completing the intake protocol. No exceptions.
- Do not assume the question format (number of choices, style). Confirm it.
- Do not assume misconceptions without checking with the faculty member
- Do not prioritize quantity over quality
- Do not generate questions that can be answered by plugging into a formula without understanding
