Case Study: Physics Department — MCQ Generation

A worked example of adapting the Workshop-in-a-Box for a department-specific session. This was tested with the Dawson College physics department.

Overview

DepartmentPhysics
ScopeAI-assisted conceptual MCQ and test question generation
Duration90 minutes, informal
ParticipantsPhysics faculty (subject matter experts)
Structure3 course-specific pods + 1 open-ended pod
ToolsChatGPT projects with custom system instructions

Why MCQ Generation?

Every physics instructor writes test questions. It’s a shared task with clear quality criteria, which makes it ideal for a focused sandbox exploration.

The specific challenge: most AI-generated physics questions default to formula-substitution or recall. Good conceptual MCQs require distractors that represent real student misconceptions. Instructors know best what those are.

This sets up a clear division of labor: the AI provides feedback on structure, variation, and pedagogical framing. The faculty member provides the physics expertise and knowledge of their students.

How It Was Set Up

Pod Structure

Faculty self-selected into groups by course in an active learning environment.

  • Pod 1: Mechanics — kinematics, dynamics, energy, momentum, torque, Newton’s laws, conservation laws
  • Pod 2: Electricity & Magnetism — electric fields/forces/potential, capacitors, circuits, magnetic fields, induction
  • Pod 3: Waves & Modern Physics — oscillations, mechanical/EM waves, Doppler effect, superposition, spectroscopy, quantum mechanics, nuclear physics, thermal radiation
  • Pod 4: Open — broader exploration (remediation design, exam wrappers, formative assessment, or any topic)

ChatGPT Projects

Each pod had a pre-configured ChatGPT project with:

  1. Shared system instructions (see below)
  2. Starter prompts as conversation starters
  3. Space to upload example questions or textbook material

System Instructions

The same system instructions were used across all four projects. The differentiation comes from what faculty bring — their course, their topics, their students’ misconceptions.

Design principles behind these instructions:

  • The AI is a physics-knowledgeable collaborator, not just a tool — it can contribute content and cite PER literature, but defers to the faculty member on what their students actually do
  • A mandatory intake protocol prevents the AI from generating before understanding the request (topic, level, misconceptions, format, purpose)
  • Question format is not assumed. The number of options, style, and structure are agreed collaboratively
  • Every output includes distractor analysis and remediation hooks

System Instructions: Physics MCQ Assessment Partner

Copy these system instructions into a ChatGPT project. They configure the AI as an assessment design partner that follows an intake protocol before generating questions.

A Note on Prompts vs. Project Setup

Most advice about using AI focuses on writing better prompts. That matters, but it’s not where the biggest difference comes from.

What made this workshop effective was the setup that happened before anyone typed anything. The system instructions, the intake protocol, the course context, the quality criteria — all of that shapes every interaction in the project. A faculty member can type a simple, natural request and get useful output because the project is already configured to know what “useful” means.

This is the difference between prompting (what you say in the conversation) and setting up useful context before the conversation starts. In this workshop, that setup is what the facilitator prepares. The starter prompts below are just entry points — the system instructions do the heavy lifting.

Starter Prompts

Shared as conversation starters:

For all pods:

  1. “I teach [mechanics / E&M / waves]. Here’s a topic my students struggle with: [topic]. What questions should I ask you before we start generating MCQs?”

  2. “Here are 3 example questions from my course. Analyze them — which ones test conceptual understanding and which are just recall? How would you improve the distractors?”

  3. “My students commonly make this error: [describe]. Help me build 3 MCQs that would reveal whether a student holds this misconception.”

  4. “Generate a scaffolded set of MCQs on [topic], easy to hard, where each question builds on the reasoning from the previous one.”

For Pod 4 (open-ended), add:

  1. “I want to explore something beyond MCQ generation — remediation design, exam wrappers, or using AI for formative assessment. Let’s talk about what I’m trying to do.”

How It Ran

The workshop was run informally. The facilitator:

  1. Introduced the sandbox concept briefly
  2. Walked through a live example using one of the pod projects — showing the intake protocol, the generation, and the distractor analysis
  3. Let faculty explore in their pods for ~45 minutes
  4. Brought groups back together for sharing and reflection

What Was Observed

Faculty engaged quickly once they had a concrete task. The pre-configured projects removed the “what do I even ask?” barrier. Faculty could start immediately with a topic they teach.

The AI’s intake questions can be valuable. Being asked “what misconceptions do you see?” guides faculty to articulate knowledge they hold implicitly. Several noted this was useful independent of the AI output.

Some older narratives surfaced but didn’t dominate. Concerns about hallucination, sycophancy, and unreliability came up, but direct experience with a well-configured tool helps shift the conversation toward “when is this useful?” rather than “can we trust it?”

Faculty see how much time goes into routine question-writing, variant generation, and rubric language. Offloading some of that to a well-configured tool lets them spend more time on the parts of teaching that need their attention.

Different courses surface different challenges, and hearing colleagues’ approaches sparked new ideas. But because everyone was working on the same type of task — assessment design — the conversation had common ground. A mechanics instructor’s insight about distractor quality was immediately relevant to someone teaching waves. That shared focus is what makes a department workshop richer than individual exploration.

What the Facilitator Learned

  • The setup matters more than the prompts. What shaped the quality of every interaction was the system instructions — the quality criteria, the intake protocol, the course context. In practice, this meant preparing the AI space around the task, not just improving the wording of the prompt. Without that setup, output was generic. With it, faculty could type simple requests and get useful results.
  • Testing before the workshop is essential. The first version of system instructions failed — the AI asked clarifying questions but then generated anyway without waiting. Iteration fixed this.
  • Faculty are the experts. This approach works because it positions faculty as collaborators, not learners. They weren’t being trained to use AI. They were evaluating whether AI could help them do something they already do well.
  • Informal beats formal for department workshops. With colleagues, a live walkthrough and open exploration was more effective than a structured slide presentation.

Adapting This for Your Department

To create your own version of this workshop:

  1. Pick your task — what’s the equivalent of “MCQ generation” in your discipline? (See the adaptation guide for ideas)
  2. Write system instructions — use the physics version above as a template. Replace the physics content with your discipline’s quality criteria, common challenges, and course context.
  3. Create some starter prompts — 4-5 entry points that help faculty start without having to engineer prompts from scratch.
  4. Test it — run through at least one project yourself. Iterate on the system instructions based on what you see.
  5. Run it informally — frame, demo, explore, share, reflect. 90 minutes is enough to get a good discussion and exploration started.
  6. Follow up — collect artifacts, gather reflections, let the department decide what to do next.
