# Sandbox Experiment

This is the hands-on exercise for Session S-06. You will move from a simple prompt to a context-rich one, and leave with one small experiment for your own context.

The session shows the sandbox in use, then has you work through the same approach yourself. As you work, you are experiencing a structure you could later run with colleagues.

## How To Work

The sandbox is a prepared set of instructions and boundaries given to an AI tool. For this session it is a shared ChatGPT you can open in the next step.

- Explore the first two steps with your table, on one device, so you build shared language. One person can drive while the table reads and discusses.
- Continue the last two steps on your own device for your individual draft. Pairs can share one laptop and swap roles.
- Each step gives you a prompt to copy into the sandbox. The exercise saves in your browser as you go.
- Do not paste student or personal information into any AI tool. Use an excerpt, summary, or invented example when needed.

## Set Up Your AI Tool

This activity runs in a prepared ChatGPT with the sandbox instructions already loaded.

**[Open the GenAI SALTISE Sandbox](https://chatgpt.com/g/g-6a1e4c09b2988191b1565ce792a521c7-genai-saltise-sandbox)** and keep it open in another tab. Sign in with a free ChatGPT account if prompted. Each step below gives you a prompt to paste into it.

Using a different AI tool? Paste the instructions below as your first message, or add them as project or custom instructions, to load the same prepared behavior.

## The Exercise

Work through the four steps in order. Pick a task, try the simple prompt, add your context and run it again, then capture what you would keep. Each step gives you a prompt to copy into your AI tool, and your work saves in this browser as you go.

<details>
<summary>Two kinds of context</summary>

Context engineering enters this exercise at two points:

- **Prepared for you.** Someone wrote the sandbox's instructions so it asks for the right information and keeps experiments small. When you run a plain request and the sandbox asks you a question instead of answering, that prepared layer is telling you what context it needs.
- **Supplied by you.** Your task, learning goal, the difficulty students hit, and your review criterion are the context only you can add. That is the second and third steps of the exercise.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What to notice</summary>

As you compare the simple prompt with the context-rich one, watch for:

- what the prepared AI tool asks before it gives advice
- where educator judgment remains necessary
- what made the response specific enough to review

If the response is still too general, add one more piece of context and run it again. A useful experiment can also show where AI is not yet helpful for the task.

</details>

Your job is to help the educator make the experiment specific enough to review. Do not sell AI. Do not argue for adoption. Do not give a long list of possible uses unless the educator asks for one.

Work from the educator's context:
- learning goal or work goal
- student or participant prior knowledge
- common misconceptions or recurring difficulties
- task, artifact, or support workflow
- source material the educator can safely share
- boundaries, academic integrity needs, and privacy limits
- review criteria and evidence of usefulness

Keep every experiment small:
- one task or artifact
- one first use
- one learner group, course, support setting, or program context
- one or two review criteria
- one reason to continue, revise, or stop

- state what the AI may help draft, compare, summarize, or question
- state what stays with the educator
- do not invent institutional policy
