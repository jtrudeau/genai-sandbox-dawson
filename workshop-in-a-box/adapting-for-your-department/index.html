Adapting the Workshop for Your Department

Run a Workshop gives you the general version. This guide shows you how to narrow it for one department, program, or shared type of task without losing the main workshop logic.

When This Version Helps Most

Use a department version when:

  • colleagues share one discipline, program, or course family
  • one task type matters more than broad exploration
  • examples from the department or program will teach more than generic ones
  • the group wants a common point of reference for later discussion

A department workshop should still stay small enough to run cleanly. The value comes from shared focus.

Start with the Same Simple Pattern

The general workshop begins with five notes:

  • task
  • context
  • what you want the result to do
  • limits
  • what needs review

Keep that pattern. Do not jump straight to a highly prepared AI setup if the group is still new to the work.

You can add more department or course material later if it clearly helps:

  • examples from the department
  • rubrics, sample questions, or short reference material
  • shared quality criteria
  • a prepared AI space with starter instructions

How to Adapt the Workshop

1. Choose one shared task

Pick one task the group already knows well. Do not frame the session as “AI in teaching” in general. Choose something specific enough that people can compare results:

  • writing multiple-choice questions
  • building practice problems or worked examples
  • drafting feedback language
  • creating case studies or scenarios
  • preparing discussion prompts

2. Prepare a short shared context

Write a short version of the task context before the workshop. This gives the group a common starting point.

For example:

  • what the task is for
  • what level or course context it belongs to
  • what you want the result to do
  • what constraints matter in the department, program, or institution
  • what still needs faculty review

3. Add department or course material only when it helps

You may want to bring:

  • sample assignments or excerpts
  • examples that show the quality bar
  • course-specific constraints
  • reference material people would normally use

Only add these if they help the task. Too much material too early can slow the workshop down.

4. Decide whether a prepared AI space is worth it

Sometimes a blank chat plus a shared context is enough. Sometimes it helps to prepare a ChatGPT project or similar space in advance.

Use a prepared AI space when:

  • the quality criteria are very specific
  • the group will benefit from common starter instructions
  • example material needs to stay in view throughout the session

If you prepare the AI space, keep the purpose plain: it should help people start faster and compare results more easily.

5. Keep faculty expertise in the lead

The AI can help generate options, variations, or drafts. Faculty still bring the important judgment:

  • what belongs in the discipline
  • what misconceptions matter
  • what quality looks like
  • what should be kept, changed, or rejected

That is the point of the workshop. People are not there to admire the tool. They are there to test whether it helps with work they already understand well.

What Facilitators Should Expect

  • skepticism is normal and worth hearing
  • the best discussion often comes from comparing results, not just generating them
  • the room may quickly move from “Can AI do this?” to “When would this actually help?”
  • shared discussion matters as much as individual outputs

Protect time for people to compare what they noticed.

After the Workshop

If the department wants to keep going, keep the follow-up simple.

Save what helped

  • save strong examples or conversations
  • note what setup choices helped most
  • keep short records of what people found worth keeping or not worth keeping

Gather quick reflections

Ask questions like:

  • Did this produce anything you would actually use?
  • What part of the setup helped most?
  • What still needed careful correction?
  • What would you change next time?

Decide what happens next

The department may decide to:

  • continue testing one task type
  • stop using AI for certain tasks
  • draft departmental guidance
  • build a small shared resource for future use

These are follow-up options, not requirements.

Getting Started

  1. choose one shared task
  2. write a short shared context
  3. gather only the materials that clearly help
  4. decide whether you need a prepared AI space
  5. test the flow yourself before the session
  6. run the workshop and protect time for comparison and discussion
