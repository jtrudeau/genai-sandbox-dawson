Adapting the Workshop for Your Department

The generic Workshop-in-a-Box gives you a cross-disciplinary framework. This guide shows you how to adapt it for a department-specific workshop which is narrower in scope, potentially more relevant, and grounded in your colleagues’ expertise.

Why Run a Department-Specific Workshop?

The generic workshop invites participants to explore AI across any teaching challenge. A department-specific version does something different. When colleagues who share a discipline sit down together with a focused task, several things become possible:

  • The department develops its own guidelines through direct experience, independent of institutional policy.
  • Both adoption and informed non-use are legitimate outcomes. The goal is not to convert anyone. It’s to be in a position to make real decisions.
  • Faculty keep authority over their discipline. The AI is a collaborator, not a replacement.
  • Direction comes from the people doing the work — departments decide what’s useful based on their own exploration, not from institutional mandate or vendor enthusiasm.
  • A shared vocabulary emerges. After a session, your department has a common reference point for talking about AI in your discipline. That’s harder to build from individual exploration.

The Adaptation Method

The generic workshop uses broad, fill-in-your-context prompts. A department adaptation can have a tighter focus:

1. Narrow the scope

Pick one specific teaching task that everyone in the department does. Don’t try to cover “AI in teaching” broadly. Try to focus on something concrete:

  • Generating assessment items (MCQs, short answer, problem sets)
  • Creating practice problems or worked examples
  • Designing feedback on student work
  • Building case studies or scenarios
  • Scaffolding difficult concepts

Faculty can compare results because they’re all working on the same type of task.

2. Pre-configure the AI

Instead of giving faculty a blank ChatGPT window and a prompt to copy-paste, set up a ChatGPT project (or equivalent) with:

  • System instructions that encode quality criteria for the task. What makes a good version of this artifact? What should the AI insist on before generating? What mistakes should it avoid?
  • Starter prompts that give faculty entry points — ready to use, but open enough for them to bring their own material.
  • Example material (uploaded) — textbook excerpts, sample questions, rubrics, or other reference material that sets the quality bar and discipline context.

The system instructions are where your pedagogical stance lives. They’re not technical — they’re a statement about what “good” looks like in your discipline.

3. Let faculty bring their expertise

The pre-configured project handles the AI collaboration framework. Faculty bring:

  • Their specific topic or course
  • The misconceptions, errors, or challenges they actually see in their students
  • Their judgment about what’s useful and what isn’t

This is the key shift: faculty are not learning to use AI. They’re using AI to do something they already know how to do, and noticing what changes.

What to Expect When You Run This

Based on tested department workshops, here are some things facilitators should be prepared for.

Faculty might be operating with incomplete information. Narratives about AI from even a year ago (hallucination, unreliability, “it just makes things up”) may no longer apply as frontier models become more capable. These concerns are real, but they can get in the way. Direct experience with a well-configured tool updates the picture faster than any presentation.

When faculty realize how many routine tasks — writing question variants, generating practice problems, creating rubric language — could be offloaded, the conversation often shifts to what matters more: what should we spend our time on instead?

Skepticism is healthy. Welcome it. Encourage faculty to push back on AI output, test the limits of what’s possible, and validate results against their own expertise. That’s the workshop working as intended.

The real value is the conversation between faculty, not the interaction with the AI. Cross-group sharing is where insights emerge. Protect time for it.

After the Workshop: Gathering Learnings

Even if the workshop is a one-off experience for your context it is a good practice to gather learnings. Below are some recommendations for what to do next.

Collect artifacts

  • Save the conversations from each sandbox project
  • Screenshot or export the best questions/materials generated
  • Note what prompts were most effective
  • Compile and share with participants if possible

Gather reflections

Ask participants (informally or via a short survey):

  • Did this produce anything you’d actually use?
  • Where did you have to correct the AI — and was that correction useful thinking?
  • What would you need to make this part of your workflow?
  • What concerns remain?

Decide as a department

After the workshop, the department has enough shared experience to have a real conversation about:

  • Are there tasks where AI assistance is clearly useful? Which ones?
  • Are there tasks where it’s clearly not appropriate? Which ones?
  • What guidelines (if any) should we adopt as a department?
  • Does anyone want to continue experimenting? How do we support that?
  • What do we want to communicate to students about our approach?

Protect autonomy and agency.

Produce department artifacts (optional)

Some departments may want to formalize what they learned:

  • A brief position statement on AI use in the discipline
  • A set of vetted, AI-assisted assessment items
  • Guidelines for when and how to use AI in course preparation
  • A shared prompt library or ChatGPT project template

These are outputs of the process, not prerequisites for it.

Getting Started

  1. Pick your scope — one task, not “everything about AI”
  2. Write system instructions — encode what “good” looks like for that task in your discipline (see the Physics case study for a template)
  3. Set up 2-4 ChatGPT projects — one per sub-group (by course, topic, or interest)
  4. Add starter prompts and example material
  5. Test it yourself — run through one project end-to-end before the workshop
  6. Run the workshop — 90 minutes is enough. Frame, demo, explore, share, reflect.
  7. Follow up — collect artifacts, gather reflections, let the department decide

The goal is not to become AI experts. It’s to free up attention for the work that matters most — helping students learn to think.
