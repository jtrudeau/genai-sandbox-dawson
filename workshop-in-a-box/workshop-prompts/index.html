Workshop Prompts

How to Use These Prompts

All prompts below are ready to copy and paste into ChatGPT (or another LLM).

For facilitators: Use Set 1 prompts to prepare your demonstration.

For participants: You’ll use Set 2 (hands-on activity) and Set 3 (brainstorming) during the workshop.

Set 1: Facilitator Demonstration Prompts

Choose ONE of these scenarios to demonstrate during the workshop. You can run this beforehand and show the output, or run it live.

Purpose: Show participants what a sandbox experiment looks like - small, specific, and tied to real teaching challenges.

Facilitator note: Modify these to match your audience’s disciplines if running a department-specific workshop.

Scenario A: Generating Discussion Prompts (Social Sciences/Humanities)

I'm a college sociology instructor preparing for a class discussion on social stratification. I typically create 3-4 discussion prompts per class, but they often feel repetitive or too broad.

Help me generate 5 alternative discussion prompts for a 75-minute class on social stratification.

Context:
- Students: Second-year college, mixed majors (intro-level sociology course)
- They've read a chapter on class, income inequality, and social mobility
- Learning goal: Students should connect abstract concepts to observable patterns in their own communities
- My usual prompts tend to be too generic (e.g., "What is social stratification?")

For each prompt, briefly explain what makes it effective for generating discussion.

I want prompts that:
- Are specific enough to spark debate but open enough for multiple perspectives
- Connect theory to students' lived experiences
- Encourage students to analyze rather than just describe
- Can be answered in 10-15 minutes of small group discussion

Expected output: 5 discussion prompts with brief rationale. Facilitator reviews these with participants, discussing what makes them “sandbox-appropriate” (specific, testable, reversible).

Scenario B: Creating Scaffolded Practice Problems (STEM)

I teach introductory calculus at a community college. Many of my students struggle with the concept of limits - they can follow my examples but freeze when trying problems on their own.

Help me create a scaffolded sequence of 4 practice problems on limits that gradually increase in difficulty.

Context:
- Topic: Finding limits algebraically (not graphically or numerically)
- Students: First-year college, many are anxious about math
- Current problem: The textbook jumps from simple examples to complex problems too quickly
- I need problems that build confidence while still challenging students

For each problem:
1. State the problem clearly
2. Indicate what new challenge this problem introduces compared to the previous one
3. Suggest one hint I could give if students get stuck

The first problem should be very straightforward. The fourth should be challenging but still solvable with the techniques we've covered.

Expected output: 4 scaffolded problems with hints and progression notes. Facilitator highlights how this could be used as a small experiment: “Try this sequence in one section, compare to your usual problems in another section.”

Scenario C: Designing Low-Stakes Reflection Prompts (Any Discipline)

I teach a professional writing course and want to incorporate more reflective practice without adding to my grading load. I'm interested in using short reflection prompts at the end of each class.

Help me create 5 different one-minute reflection prompts that encourage students to think about their learning process without requiring extensive written responses.

Context:
- Course: Professional writing (business communication, resumes, reports)
- Students: Third-year college, career-focused
- Time constraint: Last 3-5 minutes of each 90-minute class
- Goal: Encourage metacognition about writing skills, not evaluate their performance
- I want variety - not the same "What did you learn today?" every time

For each prompt:
1. Write the exact wording I would display or say to students
2. Explain what aspect of learning this prompt targets (skill development, process awareness, confidence, etc.)
3. Suggest how students might respond (whole class artifact, quick write, think-pair-share, etc.)

Keep prompts simple, specific, and genuinely useful for students to think about their progress.

Expected output: 5 varied reflection prompts with implementation suggestions. Facilitator emphasizes documentation: “You’d want to note which prompts generated the most thoughtful responses.”

Set 2: Participant Hands-On Prompt (Part 1 Activity)

For participants: Everyone will try this prompt together during the workshop. This puts you in the position of being a student using AI as a learning tool.

Purpose: Experience what it feels like to use AI in a structured, pedagogically sound way.

Activity: Brainstorm Your Own Teaching Challenge

I'm a college instructor exploring how AI might support my teaching. I want to brainstorm small, reversible experiments I could try in my course.

Help me generate 5 potential "sandbox experiments" - small, low-risk ways to experiment with AI in teaching.

Context about my teaching:
- Discipline: [INSERT YOUR DISCIPLINE OR SUBJECT AREA]
- Course level: [e.g., first-year intro, upper-level specialized, graduate, mixed]
- One thing that feels stuck, repetitive, or challenging in my teaching: [INSERT YOUR CHALLENGE - be specific]
- What I value most in teaching: [e.g., deep discussion, student agency, conceptual understanding, practical skills, etc.]

For each experiment idea:
1. Give it a clear, specific title (not generic like "Use AI for feedback")
2. Describe exactly what I would do (1-2 sentences)
3. Explain what I would learn from trying this (what question would this answer?)
4. Note one potential risk or concern to watch for

I want experiments that are:
- Small enough to try in one or two class sessions
- Reversible (I can stop if it's not working)
- Focused on student learning, not just saving me time
- Aligned with my teaching values

What to do:

  1. Fill in your context (discipline, course level, challenge, values)
  2. Copy the full prompt into ChatGPT
  3. Review the 5 experiment ideas
  4. Pick ONE that feels most relevant to you
  5. Be ready to share in small groups

Time for this activity: 10-15 minutes

Set 3: Participant Brainstorming Prompts (Part 2 Activity)

For participants: Choose the scenario below that best matches what you want to explore. Use this to generate ideas you can actually try in your teaching.

Purpose: Go deeper into one specific area and generate actionable ideas.

Facilitator note: These prompts can be modified to be more narrow for department-specific workshops (e.g., all faculty exploring multiple-choice question generation for remediation).

Scenario A: Generating Teaching Materials

Use this if you want to explore: Creating prompts, examples, practice problems, or other instructional content

I want to explore using AI to help me generate teaching materials more efficiently while maintaining quality and pedagogical soundness.

Help me design a small experiment for creating [CHOOSE ONE: discussion prompts / practice problems / worked examples / assignment variations / reading comprehension questions].

Context:
- Discipline: [YOUR DISCIPLINE]
- Specific topic or concept: [BE SPECIFIC - e.g., "photosynthesis for intro biology" not just "biology"]
- Current challenge: [What's hard about creating these materials now? Time? Variety? Quality? Accessibility?]
- How I currently create these: [Your current process]

Design an experiment where I:
1. Create materials using AI assistance
2. Compare them to my usual approach
3. Document what's better, worse, or different
4. Decide if this is worth continuing

For this experiment, provide:
- The specific prompt I should use to generate materials (write it out exactly)
- What criteria I should use to evaluate the AI-generated materials
- What evidence I should collect (screenshots, student responses, my own observations)
- One risk to watch for and how to mitigate it

Keep the experiment small - I should be able to try this in one week.

Scenario B: Providing Feedback and Assessment Support

Use this if you want to explore: Using AI to identify patterns in student work, generate feedback, or streamline assessment

I want to explore using AI to help me provide better or more efficient feedback to students.

Help me design a small experiment for [CHOOSE ONE: identifying common misconceptions / generating personalized feedback / creating rubrics / spotting patterns in student errors / providing revision suggestions].

Context:
- Discipline: [YOUR DISCIPLINE]
- Type of student work: [e.g., short essays, problem sets, lab reports, discussion posts, presentations]
- Current challenge: [What's hard about feedback now? Volume? Consistency? Depth? Timeliness?]
- What good feedback looks like in my discipline: [Be specific - what do students need to hear?]

Design an experiment where I:
1. Use AI to assist with feedback on a small sample of student work (5-10 samples)
2. Compare AI-assisted feedback to my usual feedback
3. Consider student learning impact (not just my time savings)
4. Decide what role, if any, AI should play

For this experiment, provide:
- The specific prompt I should use (include what information about students/assignment I should give the AI)
- How to ensure feedback remains pedagogically sound and discipline-appropriate
- What evidence I should collect to evaluate whether this experiment is successful
- Ethical considerations: What should I tell students? What should I NOT rely on AI for?

Keep the experiment small - I should be able to try this with one assignment.

Scenario C: Scaffolding and Supporting Struggling Students

Use this if you want to explore: Creating differentiated materials, additional support resources, or adaptive scaffolding

I want to explore using AI to better support students who are struggling with course concepts.

Help me design a small experiment for [CHOOSE ONE: creating simplified explanations / generating additional practice / building concept maps / providing just-in-time hints / differentiating materials for different skill levels].

Context:
- Discipline: [YOUR DISCIPLINE]
- Specific concept students struggle with: [BE SPECIFIC]
- What makes this concept hard: [Abstraction? Prerequisites? Terminology? Application?]
- What I currently do to support struggling students: [Your current approach]
- What I wish I had time/capacity to do: [Your ideal support]

Design an experiment where I:
1. Use AI to create scaffolding or support materials
2. Try them with a small group of students who need extra help
3. Observe whether students can use these materials productively
4. Decide if this approach helps without creating dependency

For this experiment, provide:
- The specific prompt I should use to generate support materials
- How to test whether materials actually help students understand (not just complete the work)
- What evidence I should collect (student work, confidence, follow-up performance)
- One major risk: How do I ensure students are still doing the cognitive work, not just consuming AI-generated content?

Keep the experiment small - I should be able to try this with 3-5 students over one week.

Scenario D: Brainstorming Assignment Design or Course Improvements

Use this if you want to explore: Redesigning assignments, generating new activity ideas, or refreshing course structure

I want to use AI as a brainstorming partner to improve an assignment or activity that isn't working well.

Help me redesign [CHOOSE ONE: a writing assignment / a group project / a lab activity / a discussion activity / a problem set / an assessment].

Context:
- Discipline: [YOUR DISCIPLINE]
- Current assignment/activity: [Describe what students currently do - be specific]
- What's not working: [Student engagement? Learning outcomes? Clarity? Authenticity? Too easy/hard?]
- What I want students to learn or practice: [Learning objective - be specific]
- Constraints I need to maintain: [Time, resources, class size, etc.]

Design an experiment where I:
1. Use AI to generate 3-5 alternative versions or significant modifications
2. Select one to pilot in the next offering of this course
3. Compare student work/engagement/learning to the original version
4. Decide what to keep, modify, or discard

For this experiment, provide:
- The specific prompt I should use to generate alternatives (include what context the AI needs)
- What criteria I should use to evaluate whether alternatives are actually better (not just different)
- How to pilot test this without risking student learning (try in one section? optional version? small group?)
- What evidence I should collect to make an informed decision

Keep the experiment small - I should be able to pilot one alternative in the next 2-4 weeks.

Scenario E: Student-Facing AI Use (Teaching Students to Use AI Critically)

Use this if you want to explore: Having students use AI as a tool, then teaching them to evaluate and improve outputs

I want to design an activity where students USE AI as part of their learning, then critically evaluate what it produces.

Help me design a student-facing activity where students [CHOOSE ONE: critique AI-generated content in my discipline / use AI to generate a draft then revise it / compare AI outputs to expert/peer work / identify errors or limitations in AI responses].

Context:
- Discipline: [YOUR DISCIPLINE]
- What makes something "good" or "accurate" in my field: [Discipline-specific quality markers]
- Learning goal: [What should students learn by doing this? Critical evaluation? Revision skills? Domain knowledge?]
- Student skill level: [Are they novices or do they have disciplinary expertise?]
- How much time in class: [One class period? Homework? Multi-week project?]

Design an experiment where students:
1. Use AI to generate something discipline-specific (a solution, argument, analysis, design, etc.)
2. Evaluate what the AI produced using discipline-appropriate criteria
3. Revise, correct, or improve the AI output
4. Reflect on what they learned about the discipline (not just about AI)

For this experiment, provide:
- The exact prompt students should give to AI (written so students can copy/paste)
- The evaluation rubric or criteria students should use to assess AI output
- What I should emphasize to students about citing AI, learning vs. outsourcing, and academic integrity
- What evidence I should collect to know if students actually learned disciplinary skills (not just how to use AI)

Keep the experiment small - this should be completable in 1-2 class periods or one homework assignment.

Using These Prompts During the Workshop

Part 1 (Hands-On Activity):

  • All participants use Set 2
  • Time: 10-15 minutes individual work
  • Then: Small group sharing (5-7 minutes)

Part 2 (Deeper Brainstorming):

  • Participants choose ONE prompt from Set 3 based on their interest
  • Time: 15-20 minutes individual work
  • Then: Pair or small group sharing with others who chose the same scenario (10-15 minutes)
  • Then: Whole group sharing of insights (10 minutes)

Facilitator role:

  • Circulate during individual work
  • Answer questions about how to fill in prompts
  • Encourage specificity (not “biology” → “enzyme kinetics in intro biology”)
  • Remind people to save their outputs for later reference

Facilitator Notes: Adapting These Prompts

For department-specific workshops:

If your workshop focuses on a specific goal (e.g., “all math faculty exploring problem generation”), you can:

  • Narrow Set 3 to 2-3 relevant scenarios instead of 5
  • Make prompts less generic by pre-filling discipline and context
  • Add department-specific evaluation criteria

Example adaptation for narrower focus:

Math Department Workshop: Multiple Choice Questions for Remediation

Replace Set 3 with ONE prompt:

“I teach [MATH COURSE]. I want to create a bank of multiple-choice questions that help students identify and address their misconceptions in [SPECIFIC TOPIC].

Help me design 5 MCQs where:

  • Each incorrect answer represents a common student error or misconception
  • The question requires conceptual understanding, not just procedural recall
  • I can use these for low-stakes self-assessment, not graded exams

For each question, explain what misconception each incorrect answer reveals, so I can provide targeted remediation.”

For broader institutional workshops:

Keep all 5 scenarios in Set 3 and let participants choose based on their interests. This creates natural groupings for discussion.

After the Workshop: What Participants Should Do

Immediate next steps (Week 1):

  1. Save your AI-generated ideas from today
  2. Pick ONE experiment that feels doable
  3. Refine the experiment using the sandbox template (link to faculty resources)

Try it (Weeks 2-4): 4. Run your small experiment 5. Document what happens (honestly - failures are data) 6. Collect evidence (screenshots, student responses, your observations)

Share back (end-of-semester): 7. Optional: Reconnect with today’s cohort 8. Share what you tried, what worked, what didn’t 9. Decide what to continue, modify, or stop

The goal is not to become an AI expert. The goal is to stay curious, try small things, and build judgment about what serves your students’ learning.
