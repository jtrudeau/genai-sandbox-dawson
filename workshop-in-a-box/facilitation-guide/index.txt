# Facilitation Guide

Use this guide to run the S-06 structure with colleagues. It is designed for a 75-minute session, with options for shorter or longer meetings.

The facilitator does not need to be the AI expert in the room. The facilitator's role is to keep the work grounded in real teaching and learning tasks, protect time for review, and help participants leave with one clear next decision.

## Before The Session

Prepare four things:

1. A prepared sandbox using the instructions below, seeded with the example experiments in this guide. You drive this version on screen during the examples block.
2. A way for participants to use an AI tool during the hands-on work. Either offer a sandboxed ChatGPT account or prepared project, or let participants use their own LLM. If participants use their own tool, give them the system instructions block below to paste first.
3. A participant drafting space using [Sandbox Experiment](./shared-sandbox-project) or a shared document.
4. One short example from your own context, in addition to the seeded set.

Discovery is a table activity, so one device per table is enough for that step. For the individual draft, participants work on their own laptops, and pairs can share one laptop by swapping between running the prompt and taking notes.

Keep a backup path for anyone without a device. The backup path can be simple: you run the sandbox on screen, and those participants draft on paper or in a document.

Do not paste student or personal information into any AI tool, on any path. A sandboxed account adds containment but does not change this rule.

## Prepare The Sandbox

Create one project, custom instruction set, or saved chat space called:

```text
GenAI Sandbox S-06: Experiment Designer
```

Do two things before participants arrive:

1. Add the system instructions below.
2. Seed the example experiments from the next section so the sandbox can adapt the closest one to a participant's context.

If your tool does not support project instructions, paste the system instructions as the first message in a new conversation and ask the tool to follow them for the session.

The same system instructions are the portable definition of the sandbox. Participants using their own LLM paste this block first; the participant-facing page, [Sandbox Experiment](./shared-sandbox-project), gives them a copy and the load steps.

<WorkshopPrompt
  title="System instructions for the prepared sandbox"
  description="Copy these instructions into the prepared AI space. They are written for a cross-disciplinary group of educators."
  copyHint="Copy these setup instructions into the prepared sandbox."
  prompt={`You support educators who are planning one small generative AI experiment in teaching, learning support, assessment, course design, or program work.

Your job is to help the educator make the experiment specific enough to review. Do not sell AI. Do not argue for adoption. Do not give a long list of possible uses unless the educator asks for one.

Work from the educator's context:
- learning goal or work goal
- student or participant prior knowledge
- common misconceptions or recurring difficulties
- task, artifact, or support workflow
- source material the educator can safely share
- boundaries, academic integrity needs, and privacy limits
- review criteria and evidence of usefulness

Keep every experiment small:
- one task or artifact
- one first use
- one learner group, course, support setting, or program context
- one or two review criteria
- one reason to continue, revise, or stop

Ask before drafting if important information is missing. Ask one brief question and stop.

Protect educator judgment:
- state what the AI may help draft, compare, summarize, or question

The sandbox is ready when it asks for missing educational context, keeps the experiment small, identifies what stays educator judgment, and gives a review criterion instead of generic advice. Run one or two of the examples yourself before the session to confirm.

## Room Setup

Use tables or groups of 3-5 when possible. The first activity works best when participants can talk through what they notice before drafting alone.

Each table explores one prepared sandbox together for discovery. One person can drive while the table reads and discusses what the designed context makes possible. Participants then use their own laptops for the individual draft, with the facilitator-provided ChatGPT or their own LLM.

Decide before the session:

- which prepared sandbox each table will open for discovery: your ChatGPT on a table device or a shared screen
- which AI tools participants will use for the individual draft: your sandboxed ChatGPT, their own LLM, or a mix
- how participants will avoid sharing student or personal information
- where participants will save their draft after the session

If participants are on different tools for the draft, comparing the responses can be useful, but keep the focus on the designed context rather than the tool.

## 75-Minute Run Sheet

The timing below is a suggestion for a 75-minute session including questions. Adapt it to your audience and room constraints.

| Time | Facilitator move | Participant work |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0-20 min | Frame purpose and boundaries, present the examples live in the seeded sandbox | Pick one real task or artifact worth testing |
| 20-32 min | Lead table discovery in the prepared sandbox | Explore as a table and notice what the prepared context changes |
| 32-48 min | Move to individual experiment drafting | Draft one small experiment |
| 48-60 min | Guide peer feedback | Check scope, judgment, boundaries, and review criteria |
| 60-68 min | Close with facilitation transfer | Write one facilitation technique you would reuse |
| 68-75 min | Take questions and hold buffer | Ask questions, save the draft |

## Opening Frame

Say this in your own words:

```text
Today is about testing one small use of generative AI in a teaching, support, or program context. We are not trying to cover every tool or decide whether AI belongs everywhere. We are using the sandbox to make one possible use specific enough to review.

  The educator sets the learning goal, provides the context, protects student agency, and decides what is useful. The AI can help draft, compare, question, or suggest. Disciplinary judgment stays with the educator.
```

## Table Discovery

Ask each table to open [Sandbox Experiment](./shared-sandbox-project) and work the exercise. The first two steps are a table activity: pick one task, run the simple prompt, and notice what stays generic. One person can drive while the table discusses what the designed context makes possible.

While tables work, listen for:

- tasks that are still too broad
- missing source material
- unclear student-facing boundaries
- review criteria that are too vague
- strong examples of educator judgment

Bring the room back with two questions:

1. What information did the sandbox need before it could help well?
2. What still needed educator judgment?

## Individual Drafting

Ask participants to continue the exercise on their own device: add their context one piece at a time, run the context-rich prompt, and capture the experiment.

Circulate with these questions:

- What is the first use?
- Who is this for?
- What source material will you give the AI?
- What must students still do themselves?
- What will you check before using anything?
- What would make you revise or stop?

Keep participants from expanding the scope. One task is enough.

## Peer Feedback

Use pairs or table groups. Ask the reviewer to focus on the experiment design, not on whether they personally like the AI use.

Use these peer feedback questions:

- Is the task specific?
- Is the scope small enough to try?
- Is educator judgment visible?
- Are student agency and academic integrity protected?
- Is there a clear review criterion?
- What should be answered before trying it?

Each participant should leave with one practical revision.

## Facilitation Transfer

Close by recapping the structure participants just experienced:

1. Explore a prepared sandbox together.
2. Build shared language from the group experience.
3. Draft one individual experiment.
4. Use peer feedback before treating the draft as ready.
5. Decide what to continue, revise, or stop.

Ask participants to write one facilitation technique they could reuse with colleagues.

## Shorter Session

For 45 minutes, do not try to compress everything.

Use:

- 5 minutes for framing
- 15 minutes for table discovery
- 15 minutes for a partial experiment draft
- 10 minutes for one peer feedback question and close

The outcome is one clearer starting point, not a complete draft.

## Longer Session

For 90-120 minutes, add time for:

- participants to bring course or support materials into the draft
- a second peer feedback round
- department-specific criteria
- planning how to collect evidence after the first use

- the session page

- Task: draft five questions on a topic students routinely get wrong.

2. Checkpoint feedback questions.
- Task: prepare three questions a student can ask of their own draft at a checkpoint.
- Context needed: assignment instructions, rubric language, the stage students are at.

- Stays educator judgment: whether the questions match what this assignment actually rewards.
- Review criterion: a student could act on each question without the instructor present.
- Reason to revise or stop: the questions restate the rubric instead of prompting revision.

- Task: write one short prompt for after a lab or in-class activity.
- Context needed: the activity, the learning goal, what students often miss.
- What AI helps with: a prompt that asks students to explain their reasoning, not report results.
- Stays educator judgment: whether the prompt fits the activity and the time available.
- Review criterion: responses would reveal student thinking, not yes or no answers.
- Reason to revise or stop: the prompt invites a summary rather than reasoning.

- Task: improve three questions for one reading or case.
- Context needed: the reading, the learning goal, the discussion format.
- What AI helps with: sharper questions and a sequence from concrete to interpretive.

- Review criterion: each question has more than one defensible response.
- Reason to revise or stop: the questions have a single expected answer.
