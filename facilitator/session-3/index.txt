## From Single Experiments to Sustained Inquiry

**Welcome back.** By now you've tried at least one experiment—maybe iterated on it, maybe pivoted. You're building a practice of **noticing, documenting, and adjusting**.

Today we're going to:
1. Explore what patterns are emerging from your experiments
2. Design a **second experiment** that builds on what you've learned
3. Address deeper questions about AI in your teaching

---

## Opening: Patterns Across Experiments

Let's start by looking for themes across our collective experience.

*[Facilitator: Use whiteboard or shared doc to capture themes as participants share]*

**Quick round-robin share** (1 minute per person):

Finish ONE of these sentences:

- "One thing I'm getting more confident about is..."
- "One thing I'm still puzzled by is..."
- "One pattern I'm noticing in my experiments is..."

*[Facilitator: After everyone shares, name 3-4 themes that emerged]*

## Deepening the Questions

Your first experiments were about **trying something small**. Now let's ask bigger questions—not to answer them fully, but to frame your next experiments.

### Question 1: What Is AI Actually Good For in Teaching?

Based on what you've tried so far, where does AI seem most useful?

**Common answers from sandbox participants:**

✅ **Brainstorming and ideation**
- Generating multiple options quickly (prompts, examples, assignment variations)
- Helping overcome blank-page syndrome
- Suggesting approaches you wouldn't have thought of

✅ **Pattern recognition**
- Identifying common errors across student work
- Spotting themes in open-ended responses
- Flagging misconceptions for targeted feedback

✅ **Scaffolding and personalization**
- Creating multiple versions of the same task at different levels
- Generating practice problems for struggling students
- Adapting examples for different contexts or disciplines

✅ **Critique and revision**
- Offering feedback on drafts (yours or students')
- Providing alternative phrasings or structures
- Identifying gaps or unclear reasoning

❌ **Where AI often falls short:**
- Deep disciplinary expertise
- Context-specific judgment
- Originality and genuine insight
- Emotional intelligence
- Accountability and ethical responsibility

---

### Question 2: What Are Students Learning?

When you introduce AI into your teaching, what are students actually learning?

**Two risks to watch for:**

🚨 **Risk 1: Over-reliance**
Students use AI to skip thinking, not to extend it. They can't explain the work, and learning becomes performative rather than substantive.

🚨 **Risk 2: Under-engagement**
Students dismiss AI outputs as "fake" or "low quality" and don't engage critically with them—missing the chance to develop judgment and discernment.

**The sandbox approach:**

✅ Treat AI outputs as **objects to think with**, not answers to accept
✅ Require students to **explain, critique, and revise** AI-generated content
✅ Make the AI's role **transparent and specific** - what did it generate, what did humans contribute?
✅ Assess **process and reasoning**, not just final products

---

### Question 3: What About Equity?

AI raises real equity concerns. Let's name them explicitly:

**Access disparities:**
- Not all students have equal access to AI tools (free vs. paid tiers, internet access, device quality)
- Not all students have equal familiarity with AI (some have been using it for years, others have never tried it)

**Amplification of bias:**
- AI models are trained on biased data and can perpetuate stereotypes
- AI-generated examples may not represent diverse perspectives or experiences

**Hidden labor:**
- If students rely on AI to produce work, whose thinking are we really assessing?
- Does AI advantage students who are already confident writers/thinkers?

**Sandbox responses:**

- ✅ **Make AI use optional, not required** - Don't penalize students who choose not to use it
- ✅ **Provide in-class access** - If you're asking students to use AI, make sure everyone can access it during class
- ✅ **Teach critical evaluation** - Help students recognize when AI outputs are generic, biased, or inaccurate
- ✅ **Focus on transferable skills** - Emphasize skills that matter regardless of tools (critical thinking, synthesis, revision)

## Activity 2: Design Your Second Experiment

Your second experiment can be:

- **A deeper iteration** of something you've already tried
- **A new application** of AI in a different part of your teaching
- **A student-facing experiment** where students use AI directly (if you haven't tried this yet)
- **A critical evaluation exercise** where students critique AI outputs

### Experiment Design Prompts

Use these prompts to brainstorm your second experiment. You don't have to answer all of them—pick the ones that spark ideas.

**Starting from your values:**
- How could AI help you spend more time on what you value most? (e.g., if you value deep discussion, could AI handle some prep work so you have more discussion time?)
- What would it look like to use AI in a way that aligns with your pedagogical principles?

**Starting from student learning:**
- What's a skill you want students to develop? Could AI create opportunities to practice that skill?
- What if students used AI to generate a draft, then spent their effort on critique and revision instead of creation?
- How could students learn to evaluate AI outputs in your discipline?

**Starting from your workflow:**
- What takes you a lot of time that AI could help with—not to replace your judgment, but to speed up the groundwork?
- What's a task where you'd value having multiple options to choose from?

**Starting from a problem:**
- What's a recurring challenge in your course? Could AI provide a different approach?
- Where do students get stuck? Could AI offer scaffolding?

## A Word on Ambiguity

By now you might have more questions than answers. **That's appropriate.** AI in education is emergent, contested, and context-dependent. There is no single right way.

What the sandbox gives you is **a method for navigating uncertainty**: try something small, notice what happens, adjust, and try again. That method works regardless of how the technology evolves.

## From Single Experiments to Sustained Inquiry

**Welcome back.** By now you've tried at least one experiment—maybe iterated on it, maybe pivoted. You're building a practice of **noticing, documenting, and adjusting**.

Today we're going to:
1. Explore what patterns are emerging from your experiments
2. Design a **second experiment** that builds on what you've learned
3. Address deeper questions about AI in your teaching

## Deepening the Questions

Your first experiments were about **trying something small**. Now let's ask bigger questions—not to answer them fully, but to frame your next experiments.

### Question 1: What Is AI Actually Good For in Teaching?

Based on what you've tried so far, where does AI seem most useful?

**Common answers from sandbox participants:**

✅ **Brainstorming and ideation**
- Generating multiple options quickly (prompts, examples, assignment variations)
- Helping overcome blank-page syndrome
- Suggesting approaches you wouldn't have thought of

✅ **Pattern recognition**
- Identifying common errors across student work
- Spotting themes in open-ended responses
- Flagging misconceptions for targeted feedback

✅ **Scaffolding and personalization**
- Creating multiple versions of the same task at different levels
- Generating practice problems for struggling students
- Adapting examples for different contexts or disciplines

✅ **Critique and revision**
- Offering feedback on drafts (yours or students')
- Providing alternative phrasings or structures
- Identifying gaps or unclear reasoning

❌ **Where AI often falls short:**
- Deep disciplinary expertise
- Context-specific judgment
- Originality and genuine insight
- Emotional intelligence
- Accountability and ethical responsibility

---

### Question 2: What Are Students Learning?

When you introduce AI into your teaching, what are students actually learning?

**Two risks to watch for:**

🚨 **Risk 1: Over-reliance**
Students use AI to skip thinking, not to extend it. They can't explain the work, and learning becomes performative rather than substantive.

🚨 **Risk 2: Under-engagement**
Students dismiss AI outputs as "fake" or "low quality" and don't engage critically with them—missing the chance to develop judgment and discernment.

**The sandbox approach:**

✅ Treat AI outputs as **objects to think with**, not answers to accept
✅ Require students to **explain, critique, and revise** AI-generated content
✅ Make the AI's role **transparent and specific** - what did it generate, what did humans contribute?
✅ Assess **process and reasoning**, not just final products

---

### Question 3: What About Equity?

AI raises real equity concerns. Let's name them explicitly:

**Access disparities:**
- Not all students have equal access to AI tools (free vs. paid tiers, internet access, device quality)
- Not all students have equal familiarity with AI (some have been using it for years, others have never tried it)

**Amplification of bias:**
- AI models are trained on biased data and can perpetuate stereotypes
- AI-generated examples may not represent diverse perspectives or experiences

**Hidden labor:**
- If students rely on AI to produce work, whose thinking are we really assessing?
- Does AI advantage students who are already confident writers/thinkers?

**Sandbox responses:**

- ✅ **Make AI use optional, not required** - Don't penalize students who choose not to use it
- ✅ **Provide in-class access** - If you're asking students to use AI, make sure everyone can access it during class
- ✅ **Teach critical evaluation** - Help students recognize when AI outputs are generic, biased, or inaccurate
- ✅ **Focus on transferable skills** - Emphasize skills that matter regardless of tools (critical thinking, synthesis, revision)

## Activity 2: Design Your Second Experiment

Your second experiment can be:

- **A deeper iteration** of something you've already tried
- **A new application** of AI in a different part of your teaching
- **A student-facing experiment** where students use AI directly (if you haven't tried this yet)
- **A critical evaluation exercise** where students critique AI outputs

### Experiment Design Prompts

Use these prompts to brainstorm your second experiment. You don't have to answer all of them—pick the ones that spark ideas.

**Starting from your values:**
- How could AI help you spend more time on what you value most? (e.g., if you value deep discussion, could AI handle some prep work so you have more discussion time?)
- What would it look like to use AI in a way that aligns with your pedagogical principles?

**Starting from student learning:**
- What's a skill you want students to develop? Could AI create opportunities to practice that skill?
- What if students used AI to generate a draft, then spent their effort on critique and revision instead of creation?
- How could students learn to evaluate AI outputs in your discipline?

**Starting from your workflow:**
- What takes you a lot of time that AI could help with—not to replace your judgment, but to speed up the groundwork?
- What's a task where you'd value having multiple options to choose from?

**Starting from a problem:**
- What's a recurring challenge in your course? Could AI provide a different approach?
- Where do students get stuck? Could AI offer scaffolding?

## A Word on Ambiguity

By now you might have more questions than answers. **That's appropriate.** AI in education is emergent, contested, and context-dependent. There is no single right way.

What the sandbox gives you is **a method for navigating uncertainty**: try something small, notice what happens, adjust, and try again. That method works regardless of how the technology evolves.
