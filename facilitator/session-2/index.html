Session 2: Experiment Check-in & Iteration Workshop

Welcome Back to the Sandbox

You tried something. That’s the work. Whether your experiment went exactly as planned, surprised you, or didn’t work at all—you’re here with evidence. That makes this session valuable.

Today is about honest reflection and practical iteration. We’ll share what happened, troubleshoot challenges together, and refine experiments for the next round.

Opening Round: How Did It Go?

Before we dive into details, let’s get a quick pulse.

Show of hands (or chat if virtual):

  • 🟢 Green - My experiment worked better than I expected
  • 🟡 Yellow - It was mixed—some things worked, some didn’t
  • 🔴 Red - It didn’t work, or I encountered significant problems
  • ⚪ White - I didn’t get to try it yet, but I’m ready to talk about why

No judgment zone. Red and white are just as valuable as green. We learn more from things that don’t work than from things that do.

Activity 1: Document What Actually Happened

Before we share, let’s capture what you observed. Use your experiment template from Session 1 and fill in the Reflection Space.

Reflection Prompts

Answer these honestly—this is for you first, sharing second.

1. What actually happened? Not what you hoped would happen—what did you observe? Be specific.

Examples:

  • “Students were more engaged in discussion, but 3 of them asked if the prompts came from AI”
  • “The AI-generated feedback caught grammar but missed the conceptual errors I care about”
  • “I didn’t try it because I ran out of time and felt unprepared”

2. What surprised you? What was unexpected—in a good way, bad way, or just interesting way?

3. What worked? Even if the overall experiment didn’t succeed, what part of it showed promise?

4. What didn’t work? What fell flat, backfired, or created new problems?

5. What would you change next time? If you were to try this again (or a variation), what would you do differently?

6. Is this worth continuing? Three options:

  • Keep going - This is promising, I want to refine it
  • Try something else - This taught me something, but I want to pivot
  • Pause - I need to step back and rethink

[5-7 minutes of individual writing time]

Activity 2: Small Group Sharing

[Facilitator: Arrange participants into groups of 3-4. Each person gets 7-10 minutes to share their experiment and get feedback.]

Structure for Sharing (Per Person)

1. Quick context (1 minute) What was your experiment? What were you trying to learn?

2. What happened (2-3 minutes) Share your observations—what worked, what didn’t, what surprised you.

3. Group support (4-5 minutes) The group asks questions and offers:

  • Clarifying questions - “What did students say when you told them?”
  • Observations - “It sounds like the tool worked for X but not for Y”
  • Ideas for iteration - “What if you tried this as a pre-class activity instead?”
  • Validation - “That challenge makes total sense given your course structure”

Common Challenges & Troubleshooting

Here are patterns we see often in sandbox experiments. Do any of these resonate?

Challenge 1: “Students Figured Out It Was AI”

What happened: You used AI to generate something (prompts, feedback, examples), and students called it out or seemed skeptical.

Why this matters: Transparency is one of our principles. If students detected AI without you disclosing it, that’s a trust issue.

Iteration ideas:

  • Disclose upfront next time—“I used AI to help generate these prompts, then reviewed and selected them”
  • Involve students in the process—“Here are 3 AI-generated options and 3 I wrote. Can you tell which is which? Why?”
  • Use AI as a teaching tool—“Let’s look at this AI output together. What’s useful? What’s questionable?”

Challenge 2: “The AI Output Was Wrong or Unhelpful”

What happened: AI generated something that was factually wrong, too generic, or missed the point.

Why this matters: AI is not an expert in your discipline. It generates plausible-sounding text, which is not the same as accurate or pedagogically sound content.

Iteration ideas:

  • Add more constraints to your prompt—specify audience, context, discipline-specific criteria
  • Use AI for brainstorming/drafting only, then review rigorously before using with students
  • Frame AI outputs as “to be critiqued” rather than “to be trusted”
  • Build verification into your workflow—always check claims, examples, and reasoning

Challenge 3: “I Didn’t Have Time to Try It”

What happened: Your experiment plan felt too big, too uncertain, or didn’t fit into your actual schedule.

Why this matters: If experiments don’t fit into your real workflow, they won’t happen.

Iteration ideas:

  • Shrink it - What’s the smallest possible version? (e.g., instead of a full assignment, try one question)
  • Embed it - What could you try during something you’re already doing? (e.g., use AI during office hours, not in class)
  • Set a micro-deadline - “I will try this on Tuesday at 10 am” (specific beats vague)
  • Start with observation - Instead of using AI yourself, ask students to experiment and report back

Challenge 4: “It Worked, But I’m Not Sure What I Learned”

What happened: The experiment went fine, but you don’t have clear takeaways.

Why this matters: If you can’t articulate what you learned, it’s hard to iterate or share insights with others.

Iteration ideas:

  • Be more specific about evidence - Instead of “it went well,” define what “well” means (engagement, quality of work, time saved, student feedback)
  • Ask students directly - “How did this activity feel different from usual?” Collect their observations, not just your assumptions
  • Compare - Try the same task with and without AI in different sections or weeks
  • Document - Write 3 bullet points immediately after each class—what did you notice?

Challenge 5: “Students Were Too Dependent on AI”

What happened: When you introduced AI tools, students used them as a crutch instead of thinking for themselves.

Why this matters: We want AI to support learning, not replace it.

Iteration ideas:

  • Add friction - Require students to generate their own draft FIRST, then use AI to refine
  • Require explanation - Students must explain AI outputs in their own words and cite AI as a source
  • Make AI outputs imperfect on purpose - Give students an AI-generated response with deliberate errors to find and fix
  • Use AI for critique, not creation - Students create, then ask AI to critique their work
  • Emphasize process, not product - Grade based on revision and reflection, not just final output

Activity 3: Refine or Pivot

Now that you’ve reflected and heard from others, it’s time to decide: Keep going with iteration, or pivot to something new?

Option A: Iterate on Your Current Experiment

If your experiment showed promise, refine it for the next try.

Use this template:

What I’ll keep:

[What worked that you want to preserve]

What I’ll change:

[Specific modifications for next iteration]

New evidence I’ll collect:

[How you'll know if the changes helped]

When I’ll try it:

[Specific date/time]

Option B: Pivot to a New Experiment

If you learned what you needed and want to try something different, design a new experiment.

Use the same template from Session 1:

  1. Your Experiment Goal - What are you trying to learn now?
  2. Workflow Snapshot - What will you actually do?
  3. Evidence You’ll Collect - How will you know what happened?
  4. Reflection Space - Questions for after you try it
  5. Disclosure & Context - How will you tell students?

Option C: Pause and Observe

If you’re not ready to experiment yet, choose an observation goal instead.

Examples:

  • “I’ll notice when students struggle with X and consider how AI could help or hinder”
  • “I’ll ask students: ‘How are you using AI in this class? What’s helpful? What’s not?’”
  • “I’ll try an AI tool myself for my own work (not with students) and see what I learn”

Observation is still sandbox work. You’re gathering context before committing to an experiment.

What Happens Next?

Between Now and Session 3

Your commitment:

  1. Try your refined or new experiment - Document what happens
  2. Collect specific evidence - Screenshots, notes, student feedback
  3. Reflect in writing - What worked, what didn’t, what surprised you

Support available:

  • Email facilitator with questions or challenges
  • Optional async discussion space for sharing updates
  • Mid-week check-in email with resources and encouragement

Session 2 Closing Reflection

Take 3 minutes to write:

One insight I’m taking away from today:

[Your insight]

One thing I’m going to try differently:

[Your iteration or pivot]

One thing I need support with:

[Where you're stuck or uncertain—optional to share with facilitator]

A Reminder

Failure is data. If your experiment didn’t work, you learned something important about your context, your students, or the tool. That’s not wasted time—that’s exactly what sandbox work is for.

Iteration > perfection. You’re not trying to find the perfect solution in one try. You’re building understanding through repeated, small experiments.

See you at Session 3, where we’ll design second experiments and explore deeper pedagogical questions.

Next session: With one round of experiments under your belt, we’ll explore how to expand your practice and design more ambitious (but still reversible) experiments.
