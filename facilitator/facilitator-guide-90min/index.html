🎓 For Facilitators🎯 90-Minute Workshop Guide

Facilitator Guide: 90-Minute Sandbox Workshop

About This Guide

This guide is for you, the facilitator—the person creating the conditions where faculty can experiment safely, reflect honestly, and learn from each other.

The 90-minute workshop content is written for participants. This guide is the meta-layer: how to prepare, facilitate, adapt, and extend the workshop for your context.

Your Role as Facilitator

This is not a training session. You’re facilitating a learning community where faculty develop their own practice through experimentation.

Your job:

  • Create psychological safety for trying and failing
  • Ask good questions (not provide all answers)
  • Connect participants to each other
  • Model curiosity and humility
  • Guide the process, not the content

NOT your job:

  • Be an AI expert
  • Have perfect answers
  • Convince skeptics
  • Fix everyone’s problems
  • Police what people do

Core Workshop: 90-Minute Structure

Overview

Part 1 (~20 min): Framing + Demonstration

  • What is the sandbox?
  • Facilitator demonstrates with one use case
  • Transition to hands-on activity

Part 2 (~50 min): Discovery & Sharing

  • Activity 1: Everyone tries hands-on brainstorming (10-15 min)
  • Small group sharing (5-7 min)
  • Activity 2: Choose scenario, go deeper (15-20 min)
  • Group sharing by scenario (10-15 min)
  • Whole group insights (10 min)

Part 3 (~20 min): Next Steps & Resources

  • What you’ve done today
  • What to do next (week 1, weeks 2-4, end-of-semester)
  • Resources and community options
  • Final reflection (5 min)

Preparation Checklist

2-3 Weeks Before

  • Set date, time, location (or Zoom link)
  • Send invitation emails using communication templates
  • Recruit 5-30 participants (ideal: 10-20)
  • Review all workshop prompts
  • Choose which Set 1 prompt you’ll demonstrate
  • Run your chosen demonstration prompt in ChatGPT - save the output

1 Week Before

  • Send reminder email
  • Confirm room setup or test Zoom
  • Prepare materials:
    • Links to workshop prompts document
    • Links to faculty resources
    • Timer for activities
    • Whiteboard or shared doc for capturing insights
  • If in-person: Print workshop prompts doc (optional backup)

Day Before

  • Send 24-hour reminder
  • Review the 90-minute workshop content one more time
  • Test all links
  • Prepare your opening remarks (see below)

Day Of

  • Arrive 15 minutes early (in-person) or open Zoom 10 minutes early
  • Set up space for small group work
  • Have prompts document link ready to share in chat
  • Take a breath - you’ve got this

Facilitation Notes by Section

Opening (First 5 minutes)

What to do:

  1. Welcome people as they arrive (names, small talk)
  2. Start on time (respect people’s schedules)
  3. Brief intro: Your name, your role, why you’re facilitating this

Opening remarks template:

“Welcome. I’m [name], and I’m here to facilitate this session—not because I’m an AI expert, but because I believe faculty need space to explore AI thoughtfully, together.

This isn’t a training about tools. It’s an invitation to experiment. Over the next 90 minutes, you’ll experience using AI as a learning tool, brainstorm experiments for your own teaching, and connect with colleagues asking similar questions.

Three things to know:

  1. No judgment zone - Skepticism is welcome. So is curiosity. So is confusion.
  2. Small experiments - We’re not asking you to transform your teaching. Just try one small thing.
  3. Peer learning - The best insights will come from each other, not from me.

Let’s get started.”

Avoid:

  • Long introductions or icebreakers (save time for the real work)
  • Overselling AI or being overly cautious
  • Setting yourself up as the expert

Part 1: Framing + Demonstration (~20 min)

Your goal: Help participants understand what the sandbox approach is through both explanation and example.

Follow the workshop content for framing (read it beforehand so you can present naturally, not read verbatim).

Key points to emphasize:

  • Sandbox = small, reversible, documented, reflective
  • Principles: Humanism, responsibility, transparency
  • Good experiments are specific and modest

Demonstration (10 min):

  1. Show the prompt you chose from Set 1 (display on screen or read aloud)
  2. Show the AI output you generated beforehand
  3. Discuss with participants:
    • “What do you notice about this output?”
    • “What seems useful? What seems questionable?”
    • “If you were going to try this, what would you need to document?”
    • “What risks or concerns come to mind?”

Why run the prompt beforehand:

  • Saves time during workshop
  • Lets you prepare thoughtful questions about the output
  • Avoids technical glitches during live demo
  • You can still show the prompt and explain what you asked for

Optional: Run it live if:

  • You’re very comfortable with ChatGPT
  • You have reliable internet
  • Your audience would benefit from seeing the process

Transition (2-3 min):

  • “You just saw an example. Now you’ll try it yourself.”
  • Share the link to workshop prompts
  • Direct everyone to Set 2

Part 2: Discovery & Sharing (~50 min)

Activity 1: Hands-On Brainstorming (10-15 min)

What participants do:

  • Fill in Set 2 prompt with their context
  • Copy into ChatGPT
  • Review the 5 ideas
  • Pick one that resonates
  • Take notes

Your role:

  • Give clear instructions at the start
  • Set a timer (show it on screen if virtual)
  • Circulate (in-person) or monitor chat (virtual)
  • Answer questions about filling in the prompt
  • Give time warnings (5 min left, 2 min left, wrap up)

Common questions you’ll get:

  • “What if I don’t have ChatGPT?” → Use Claude or another LLM; the prompt will work with any
  • “Can I change the prompt?” → Yes, adapt it to fit your needs
  • “What if the AI gives bad ideas?” → That’s part of the learning - we’ll discuss what to do with unhelpful outputs

Troubleshooting:

  • If someone is stuck on filling in context: “What’s one specific challenge you’re facing in teaching? Start there.”
  • If someone doesn’t like any of the 5 ideas: “That’s fine - pick the least bad one, or try running the prompt again with different wording.”

Small Group Sharing (5-7 min)

How to set this up:

  • In-person: “Find 2-3 people near you”
  • Virtual: Use breakout rooms (3-4 people per room)

Instructions to give: “Each person shares for 90 seconds:

  • Which experiment idea you picked
  • Why it resonated
  • One question or concern

As listeners: What sounds promising? What would you be curious to know?”

Your role:

  • Time the activity
  • If in-person: Circulate and listen (don’t intervene unless asked)
  • If virtual: Pop into breakout rooms briefly to check energy
  • Bring everyone back on time

Activity 2: Go Deeper (15-20 min)

What participants do:

  • Choose one scenario from Set 3
  • Fill in context and run the prompt
  • Review more detailed output
  • Take notes on usability, concerns, adaptations needed

Your role:

  • Direct them to Set 3
  • Explain the 5 scenarios briefly (they’re described in the prompts doc)
  • Emphasize: “Pick the one that matches what you want to explore”
  • Set timer for 15-20 minutes
  • Circulate and support

Watch for:

  • People choosing scenarios that don’t fit their interests (redirect: “What are you actually curious about?”)
  • People spending too long reading scenarios (nudge: “Pick one and dive in - you can always try another later”)
  • Technical issues (have backup: they can read the scenarios and brainstorm without AI if needed)

Group Sharing by Scenario (10-15 min)

How to set this up:

  • “Now we’re going to regroup by scenario”
  • Option A (smaller groups): “Everyone who chose Scenario A, gather here. Scenario B over there,” etc.
  • Option B (virtual/larger groups): Assign breakout rooms by scenario

Instructions to give: “In your scenario group, discuss:

  1. What ideas emerged across different disciplines?
  2. Common challenges or concerns?
  3. What would make this experiment doable?
  4. What support would help you try this?

Choose ONE key insight to share with the whole group.”

Your role:

  • Help people find their groups
  • Time the discussion (10 min max)
  • If groups are very uneven (e.g., 1 person in Scenario C, 15 in Scenario A), you can merge smaller groups: “Scenarios C and D, work together”

Whole Group Insights (10 min)

What to do:

  • Bring everyone back
  • Each scenario group shares their one key insight (2 min per group)
  • Capture insights on whiteboard or shared doc
  • After all groups share, reflect back themes you heard

Sample reflection:

“I’m hearing a few themes:

  • Many of you are concerned about time - these experiments need to fit into already-full schedules
  • There’s curiosity about student-facing use, but also worry about over-reliance
  • Discipline-specific quality matters - AI outputs need expert review

These are exactly the right concerns to bring to your experiments.”

Avoid:

  • Letting one group dominate
  • Trying to solve every problem raised
  • Rushing this section (it’s valuable)

Part 3: Next Steps & Resources (~20 min)

Follow the workshop content for this section. Key elements:

What you’ve done today (2-3 min)

  • Summarize briefly
  • Emphasize the sandbox mindset

What to do next (5-7 min)

  • Walk through the three timeframes: this week, next 2-4 weeks, end-of-semester
  • Show where to find templates and resources
  • Point to faculty experiment template

Resources (3-5 min)

  • Briefly tour the key resources (don’t read every bullet)
  • Emphasize: “These are here when you need them”

Community options (3-5 min)

  • Explain follow-up options (cohort check-in, partner system, department exploration)
  • Gauge interest: “How many would be interested in an optional check-in at end-of-semester?”
  • Collect contact info if offering cohort continuation

Final Reflection (5 min)

  • Give actual quiet time for writing
  • Make sharing optional
  • Invite 2-3 volunteers to share one thing

Closing (2 min)

  • Thank participants
  • Final encouragement
  • Reminder of next steps

After the Workshop

Same Day

  • Send thank-you email with links to resources (use communication templates)
  • Make notes about what worked and what to adjust

Within 1 Week

  • If offering cohort continuation, send details about end-of-semester check-in
  • File away participant list for follow-up

End of Semester (if applicable)

  • Send reminder about optional check-in session
  • Host informal sharing session (60-90 min)
  • Collect stories of what people tried

Adapting the 90-Minute Workshop

Shorter Version (60 minutes)

Compress to:

  • Part 1: 15 min (shorter demo discussion)
  • Part 2: 35 min (skip Activity 1 OR Activity 2, not both)
  • Part 3: 10 min (quick resources tour, skip written reflection)

What to cut:

  • Small group sharing after Activity 1 (go straight to Activity 2)
  • Or: Skip Activity 2 entirely and just do Activity 1 + sharing

Tradeoff: Less depth, but still gives faculty hands-on experience

Longer Version (2 hours)

Expand to:

  • Part 1: 25 min (more discussion of demonstration)
  • Part 2: 70 min (more time for both activities + richer sharing)
  • Part 3: 25 min (add small group action planning)

What to add:

  • After Activity 1: Whole-group discussion of what surprised people
  • After Activity 2: More time for scenario groups to develop ideas collaboratively
  • Part 3: Pair up to create specific action plans with accountability partners

Benefit: More community building, deeper exploration

Department-Specific Version

For a single department or discipline:

Customize:

  • Set 1: Choose demonstration relevant to that discipline
  • Set 3: Narrow to 2-3 most relevant scenarios (remove others)
  • Examples: Use discipline-specific language and contexts throughout

Example: Math Department Workshop

  • Demo: Scaffolded problem generation (Set 1, Scenario B)
  • Activity 2: Offer only Scenarios A (generating materials), B (feedback), and C (scaffolding)
  • All examples and discussion reference math teaching specifically

Benefit: More targeted, faculty can go deeper into disciplinary applications

Tradeoff: Less cross-disciplinary insight

Facilitation Skills: Creating Psychological Safety

Psychological safety is the foundation. Without it, faculty won’t share honestly, take risks, or admit when things don’t work.

How to build it:

✅ Model vulnerability

  • Share your own uncertainties: “I don’t have this figured out either”
  • Admit when you don’t know: “That’s a great question - does anyone have experience with that?”

✅ Normalize failure

  • When someone shares a concern: “That’s a really important concern”
  • If someone’s experiment didn’t work: “That’s valuable data - what did you learn?”

✅ Make sharing optional

  • “Anyone want to share?” not “Let’s go around the circle”
  • Accept silence - not everyone needs to speak

✅ Respond without judgment

  • Thank people for sharing, especially difficult things
  • Avoid “yes, but…” responses

✅ Protect confidentiality

  • “What’s shared here stays here unless someone gives permission to share externally”

✅ Name discomfort

  • “This is a controversial topic and there are real disagreements - that’s okay”

What undermines safety:

  • ❌ Dismissing concerns (“Don’t worry about that”)
  • ❌ Positioning yourself as the expert with all answers
  • ❌ Comparing participants to each other
  • ❌ Rushing through sharing time
  • ❌ Letting one person dominate
  • ❌ Failing to address harmful comments

Facilitation Skills: Asking Good Questions

Your most important tool is questions, not answers.

For opening up thinking:

  • “What are you noticing?”
  • “What surprised you?”
  • “What are you curious about?”
  • “What would you try if you knew it would work?”

For deepening reflection:

  • “What does that tell you about your students/discipline/teaching?”
  • “How does this connect to what you value in your teaching?”
  • “What assumptions are you holding?”

For supporting peers:

  • “What questions does this raise for you?”
  • “What resonates with your experience?”
  • “What would you be curious to try based on what you just heard?”

For troubleshooting:

  • “What part of this is in your control?”
  • “What would make this easier or more realistic?”
  • “What’s the smallest version you could try?”

Avoid:

  • Questions that have one right answer
  • Leading questions that push your agenda
  • “Why didn’t you…” questions (sounds like blame)

Navigating Difficult Moments

Challenge: Someone is very skeptical or resistant

What to do:

  • Acknowledge concerns as legitimate: “Those are real concerns”
  • Ask: “What would need to be true for you to feel differently?”
  • Emphasize experiments are optional and reversible
  • Pair them with someone who shares concerns but is willing to try

What NOT to do:

  • Try to convince them AI is good
  • Dismiss their concerns as uninformed
  • Force participation

Challenge: Someone dominates discussion

What to do:

  • Use structured turn-taking: “Let’s hear from someone who hasn’t spoken yet”
  • Break into smaller groups where one person can’t dominate
  • Speak privately: “I appreciate your contributions - can you help me make space for quieter voices?”

What NOT to do:

  • Let it continue
  • Embarrass them publicly

Challenge: Technical issues (ChatGPT down, poor internet, etc.)

What to do:

  • Have a backup: Participants can read scenarios and brainstorm manually
  • Offer to send prompts after the workshop for people to try later
  • Focus on the thinking, not the tool: “Even without AI running, what experiments are you considering?”

What NOT to do:

  • Panic or let it derail the whole session
  • Spend 20 minutes troubleshooting tech

Challenge: Someone shares something problematic

Example: “I’m just going to have AI write all my feedback from now on”

What to do:

  • Pause and name the concern: “I want to pause here - that raises questions for me about…”
  • Invite the group: “What concerns does this raise?”
  • Redirect to principles: “How does this align with our principle of responsibility?”

What NOT to do:

  • Let it pass without comment (silence = endorsement)
  • Attack the person

Challenge: Most people didn’t engage with the activities

What to do:

  • Check in: “I’m noticing low energy - what’s going on?”
  • Adjust: “Let’s try this differently…”
  • Acknowledge honestly: “This activity isn’t landing - let’s pivot to discussion”

What NOT to do:

  • Pretend everything is fine
  • Blame participants

Extended Formats

Multi-Session Series (4 weeks)

The original 4-session workshop materials can be found in the workshop files. This extended format includes:

  • Session 1 (90 min): Design first experiment
  • Session 2 (60-90 min): Check-in and iteration
  • Session 3 (60-90 min): Expanding practice
  • Session 4 (90 min): Reflection and sustaining

When to use: When you want deeper engagement and multiple rounds of experimentation

See: The individual session files and extended facilitator guidance for full details

Half-Day Workshop (3-4 hours)

Structure:

  • Morning: Full 90-minute workshop
  • Break (30 min)
  • Afternoon: Deep dive into one topic (assessment, student-facing use, equity, etc.)
  • Closing: Action planning in pairs

When to use: Faculty development day, intersession intensive

Semester-Long Cohort

Structure:

  • Kick-off: 90-minute workshop
  • Monthly check-ins (60 min each, 3-4 over semester)
  • Final sharing session (90 min)
  • Async discussion space between meetings

When to use: Building sustained community of practice

FAQs for Facilitators

Q: Do I need to be an AI expert?

A: No. You need to be good at facilitating peer learning and creating psychological safety. Participants will teach each other about AI.

Q: What if participants ask technical questions I can’t answer?

A: Say “I don’t know - does anyone have experience with this?” or “Great question - let’s add it to our list to explore.” You’re facilitating inquiry, not delivering expertise.

Q: What if my institution has restrictive AI policies?

A: Acknowledge the constraints. Focus on experiments within policy boundaries. Frame as “policy-compliant experimentation.” If policies are unclear, use the workshop to surface questions and advocate for clarity.

Q: How do I handle very different levels of AI experience?

A: Pair experienced users with novices as resources, not experts. Emphasize that everyone is experimenting and learning regardless of technical skill.

Q: What if no one continues experimenting after the workshop?

A: That’s feedback. Reflect: Was psychological safety present? Did experiments feel doable? Were resources clear? Adjust for next time. Not everyone will continue, and that’s okay.

Q: Can I run this alone or do I need a co-facilitator?

A: You can run it alone for groups up to 15. For larger groups or if you’re new to facilitation, a co-facilitator helps with logistics, monitoring chat, and providing support.

Final Thoughts

You don’t need to be perfect. Model what it looks like to experiment, learn, and adjust.

Your most important job is the container - create space where people feel safe to try, fail, and share.

Trust the process. The workshop structure works. Follow it, adapt as needed, and trust that participants will generate their own insights.

Good luck. You’ve got this.
