23:T264f,# Session 1: Welcome to the Sandbox

## Your First Experiment Begins Now

**Welcome.** You're here because you're curious about generative AI in teaching—maybe excited, maybe skeptical, maybe both. That's exactly right.

This isn't a workshop about AI tools or prompt engineering tricks. This is a **sandbox**—a space where you get to experiment, document what happens, and reflect on what it means for your teaching. No pressure to have it figured out. No judgment if things don't work. Just curiosity, small experiments, and honest reflection.

---

## What Is a Sandbox?

Think of a sandbox as a contained space for trying things without high stakes. In this context, it means:

**Small experiments** - Not overhauling your whole course, just trying one thing
**Reversible** - You can always stop or change direction
**Documented** - You'll write down what happened, not just what you hoped would happen
**Reflective** - The learning is in noticing what emerges, not just executing a plan

### Our Guiding Principles

**Humanism** - Students are humans, not efficiency problems to solve
**Responsibility** - We acknowledge risks, consider consequences, and act with care
**Transparency** - We're honest with students about what we're trying and why

These aren't abstract ideals. They're practical guardrails that help us experiment thoughtfully.

---

## Before We Start: Setting Intentions

Take a moment to consider:

**What brought you here today?**
Not what you think you should say—what's actually on your mind? Write it down. This is for you, not for sharing (unless you want to).

*[Space for reflection - 2 minutes of quiet writing]*

**What are you worried about?**
AI in education brings up real concerns: academic integrity, equity, job displacement, quality of learning. Name yours. Acknowledging concerns doesn't mean we ignore them—it means we can address them directly.

**What are you curious about?**
Even if you're skeptical, what's one thing you're wondering? What would you want to know if the risks were managed?

*[Optional: Facilitator invites 2-3 volunteers to share ONE thing they're curious about—no pressure to share concerns publicly unless someone offers]*

---

## Activity 1: The Experiment Template

### Your Tool for Documenting Experiments

You're going to design a small experiment today. Not implement it—just plan it. The template you'll use has five parts:

1. **Your Experiment Goal** - What are you trying to learn or test?
2. **Workflow Snapshot** - What will you actually do? (Step by step)
3. **Evidence You'll Collect** - How will you know what happened?
4. **Reflection Space** - After you try it, what did you notice?
5. **Disclosure & Context** - How will you explain this to students?

Let's walk through an example together, then you'll draft your own.

---

### Example: Testing AI-Generated Discussion Prompts

**Goal:**
I want to see if Claude can help me generate more varied discussion prompts for my intro sociology course. Right now I feel like my prompts are repetitive—I want to test whether AI can give me fresh angles without losing the disciplinary rigor.

**Workflow:**
1. Pick next week's topic (social stratification)
2. Write 3 discussion prompts the way I usually do
3. Ask Claude to generate 5 alternative prompts for the same topic, specifying my course level and learning objectives
4. Compare the AI-generated prompts to mine—check for accuracy, depth, and variety
5. Select the best 3 (mine or AI or hybrid) to use in class
6. Observe discussion quality—note student engagement, depth of responses, any confusion

**Evidence:**
- Screenshots of my original prompts and AI-generated alternatives
- Notes during class discussion (engagement level, quality of student responses)
- One-minute reflection from students at end of discussion: "What made today's discussion work or not work for you?"

**Reflection (After Trying It):**
*[To be filled in after the experiment]*
- What surprised me?
- What worked better or worse than I expected?
- What would I change next time?
- Is this worth continuing?

**Disclosure:**
I'll tell students at the start of the discussion: "I've been experimenting with using AI to help me generate discussion prompts. Today's questions were created with AI assistance, then reviewed and selected by me. I'm curious whether these spark different kinds of conversations—let me know at the end how today's discussion felt."

---

### What Makes This a Good Sandbox Experiment?

- **Small & specific** - Just discussion prompts, just one class session
- **Reversible** - If it doesn't work, I go back to my usual approach next week
- **Clear evidence** - I'll know what happened because I'm collecting specific observations
- **Transparent** - Students know what I'm trying
- **Reflective** - The value is in noticing what emerges, not just "did it work"

---

## Activity 2: Design Your First Experiment

### Starter Prompts to Help You Begin

You don't need a fully formed idea. Start with one of these prompts—see where it takes you.

**Option A: Start with a frustration**
What's one thing in your teaching that feels stuck, repetitive, or draining? Could a small AI experiment give you new options?

*Examples:*
- "I spend hours grading the same conceptual mistakes over and over"
- "My students struggle to start research—they freeze at the topic selection phase"
- "I feel like I give the same feedback every semester"

**Option B: Start with a curiosity**
What's one thing you've been wondering about AI in your discipline?

*Examples:*
- "Can AI help students brainstorm without doing the thinking for them?"
- "Could AI-generated examples help students see patterns faster?"
- "What would happen if I let students critique AI-generated outputs in my field?"

**Option C: Start with what you already do**
Pick one small, regular teaching task. What if you tried it with AI assistance just once?

*Examples:*
- Creating a reading guide
- Writing a mid-semester check-in survey
- Generating practice problems
- Drafting a rubric for a new assignment

---

- What questions do you have?
- What risks or concerns come to mind?
- What would make this even more interesting?

**As the designer:**
- Listen for what resonates
- Notice where you feel uncertain
- Refine based on feedback

### Between Now and Session 2

**Your commitment** (choose what feels right):

1. **Try your experiment** - Implement it and collect evidence
2. **Refine and prepare** - If you're not ready to try it yet, refine your plan and set a specific date
3. **Start smaller** - If your experiment feels too big, scale it down and try a micro-version

**You'll document:**
- What actually happened (not what you hoped would happen)
- What surprised you
- What you learned
- What you'd change

- Email facilitator with questions

This is not about becoming an AI expert. This is about **staying curious** about your teaching, trying small things, and noticing what happens.

You're not alone in this. We're all experimenting together. Some experiments will work. Some won't. All of them will teach us something.

8d:T264f,# Session 1: Welcome to the Sandbox

## Your First Experiment Begins Now

**Welcome.** You're here because you're curious about generative AI in teaching—maybe excited, maybe skeptical, maybe both. That's exactly right.

This isn't a workshop about AI tools or prompt engineering tricks. This is a **sandbox**—a space where you get to experiment, document what happens, and reflect on what it means for your teaching. No pressure to have it figured out. No judgment if things don't work. Just curiosity, small experiments, and honest reflection.

---

## What Is a Sandbox?

Think of a sandbox as a contained space for trying things without high stakes. In this context, it means:

**Small experiments** - Not overhauling your whole course, just trying one thing
**Reversible** - You can always stop or change direction
**Documented** - You'll write down what happened, not just what you hoped would happen
**Reflective** - The learning is in noticing what emerges, not just executing a plan

### Our Guiding Principles

**Humanism** - Students are humans, not efficiency problems to solve
**Responsibility** - We acknowledge risks, consider consequences, and act with care
**Transparency** - We're honest with students about what we're trying and why

These aren't abstract ideals. They're practical guardrails that help us experiment thoughtfully.

---

## Before We Start: Setting Intentions

Take a moment to consider:

**What brought you here today?**
Not what you think you should say—what's actually on your mind? Write it down. This is for you, not for sharing (unless you want to).

*[Space for reflection - 2 minutes of quiet writing]*

**What are you worried about?**
AI in education brings up real concerns: academic integrity, equity, job displacement, quality of learning. Name yours. Acknowledging concerns doesn't mean we ignore them—it means we can address them directly.

**What are you curious about?**
Even if you're skeptical, what's one thing you're wondering? What would you want to know if the risks were managed?

*[Optional: Facilitator invites 2-3 volunteers to share ONE thing they're curious about—no pressure to share concerns publicly unless someone offers]*

## Activity 1: The Experiment Template

### Your Tool for Documenting Experiments

You're going to design a small experiment today. Not implement it—just plan it. The template you'll use has five parts:

1. **Your Experiment Goal** - What are you trying to learn or test?
2. **Workflow Snapshot** - What will you actually do? (Step by step)
3. **Evidence You'll Collect** - How will you know what happened?
4. **Reflection Space** - After you try it, what did you notice?
5. **Disclosure & Context** - How will you explain this to students?

Let's walk through an example together, then you'll draft your own.

---

### Example: Testing AI-Generated Discussion Prompts

**Goal:**
I want to see if Claude can help me generate more varied discussion prompts for my intro sociology course. Right now I feel like my prompts are repetitive—I want to test whether AI can give me fresh angles without losing the disciplinary rigor.

**Workflow:**
1. Pick next week's topic (social stratification)
2. Write 3 discussion prompts the way I usually do
3. Ask Claude to generate 5 alternative prompts for the same topic, specifying my course level and learning objectives
4. Compare the AI-generated prompts to mine—check for accuracy, depth, and variety
5. Select the best 3 (mine or AI or hybrid) to use in class
6. Observe discussion quality—note student engagement, depth of responses, any confusion

**Evidence:**
- Screenshots of my original prompts and AI-generated alternatives
- Notes during class discussion (engagement level, quality of student responses)
- One-minute reflection from students at end of discussion: "What made today's discussion work or not work for you?"

**Reflection (After Trying It):**
*[To be filled in after the experiment]*
- What surprised me?
- What worked better or worse than I expected?
- What would I change next time?
- Is this worth continuing?

**Disclosure:**
I'll tell students at the start of the discussion: "I've been experimenting with using AI to help me generate discussion prompts. Today's questions were created with AI assistance, then reviewed and selected by me. I'm curious whether these spark different kinds of conversations—let me know at the end how today's discussion felt."

---

### What Makes This a Good Sandbox Experiment?

- **Small & specific** - Just discussion prompts, just one class session
- **Reversible** - If it doesn't work, I go back to my usual approach next week
- **Clear evidence** - I'll know what happened because I'm collecting specific observations
- **Transparent** - Students know what I'm trying
- **Reflective** - The value is in noticing what emerges, not just "did it work"

- Creating a reading guide
