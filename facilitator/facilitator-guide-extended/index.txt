23:T5820,# Facilitator Guide: Running the Sandbox Workshop

## About This Guide

This guide is for **you, the facilitator**—the person responsible for creating the conditions where faculty can experiment safely, reflect honestly, and learn from each other.

The workshop content (Sessions 1-4) is written as **live-flow material** for participants. This guide is the **meta-layer**: how to adapt, facilitate, and sustain the workshop in your specific context.

---

## Workshop Overview

### Philosophy & Approach

**This is not a training.** This is a **learning community** where faculty develop their own practice through experimentation.

**Core principles:**
- **Psychological safety** - Faculty need to feel safe trying things that might fail
- **Peer learning** - The best insights come from each other, not the facilitator
- **Practice-based** - Learning happens through doing, not just discussing
- **Iterative** - Small experiments, honest reflection, continuous adjustment
- **Contextual** - What works depends on discipline, students, and teaching style

**Your role as facilitator:**
- Create the container for safe experimentation
- Ask good questions
- Normalize failure as learning
- Connect participants to each other
- Model curiosity and humility

**Not your role:**
- Be an AI expert
- Have all the answers
- Fix everyone's problems
- Convince skeptics
- Police what people do

---

## Workshop Structure

### Recommended Format

**4 sessions over 4-6 weeks**

- **Session 1** (90 minutes): Welcome & Design First Experiment
- **Session 2** (60-90 minutes): Check-in & Iteration Workshop
- **Session 3** (60-90 minutes): Expanding Your Practice
- **Session 4** (90 minutes): Reflection, Sharing & Sustaining

**Between-session work:** 2-4 hours per week trying experiments

**Total time commitment:** ~20-25 hours over 4-6 weeks

---

### Why This Structure?

**Session 1 is longer** because participants need time to:
- Build trust and psychological safety
- Understand the sandbox philosophy
- Design their first experiment thoughtfully

**Sessions 2-3 can be shorter** because:
- Participants already understand the process
- Much of the learning happens between sessions
- Sessions are for sharing and troubleshooting, not lecturing

**Session 4 is longer** to create space for:
- Reflection on the full journey
- Sharing significant insights
- Planning for sustainability
- Closing with intention

---

## Adapting This Workshop

### What to Keep (Core Elements)

These are **non-negotiable** for maintaining the sandbox philosophy:

✅ **Psychological safety** - Make it safe to fail and share honestly
✅ **Small experiments** - No pressure to transform teaching overnight
✅ **Documentation** - Use the experiment template to capture observations
✅ **Reflection** - Regular opportunities to make meaning of experiences
✅ **Peer learning** - Participants learn from each other, not just from you
✅ **Iteration** - Experiments are starting points, not endpoints

---

### What to Customize (Flexible Elements)

◯ **Timeline** - Adjust to your academic calendar
◯ **Session length** - Shorten or extend based on your context
◯ **Group size** - Works for cohorts of 5-25 (see guidance below)
◯ **Format** - In-person, virtual, or hybrid
◯ **Examples** - Use discipline-specific examples relevant to your participants
◯ **Communication style** - Adapt tone and language to your institutional culture

---

## Context-Specific Adaptations

### Timeline Variations

**Intensive format** (2 weeks):
- 4 sessions over 2 weeks (2 sessions per week)
- Between-session work: 3-5 days
- **Best for:** Intersession periods, faculty development weeks
- **Challenge:** Less time for iteration; experiments may feel rushed

**Standard format** (4-6 weeks):
- 4 sessions over 4-6 weeks (1 session per week or biweekly)
- Between-session work: 1-2 weeks
- **Best for:** Regular semester, sustained community building
- **Challenge:** Maintaining momentum across longer gaps

**Extended format** (8-10 weeks):
- 4 sessions spread across a full semester
- Between-session work: 2-3 weeks
- **Best for:** Deep exploration, multiple iterations per experiment
- **Challenge:** Participants may lose continuity; need stronger between-session support

---

### Group Size Considerations

**Small groups** (5-10 participants):
- **Advantages:** More intimate sharing, deeper discussion, easier facilitation
- **Approach:** Whole-group discussions, paired feedback, minimal breakouts
- **Facilitation notes:** You can give more individual attention; surface everyone's voice

**Medium groups** (10-20 participants):
- **Advantages:** More diversity of experience, still manageable for connection
- **Approach:** Mix of whole-group and small-group activities, use breakout rooms
- **Facilitation notes:** Plan for structured small-group time; use breakouts strategically

**Large groups** (20-30+):
- **Advantages:** Reaches more faculty, more examples to learn from
- **Approach:** Use learning stations, breakout rooms, asynchronous components
- **Facilitation notes:** Consider co-facilitators; use async discussion boards between sessions; prioritize small-group interaction over whole-group sharing

---

### Format Options

**In-person:**
- **Advantages:** Easier to build trust, better for paired activities, more spontaneous discussion
- **Logistics:** Book accessible room with moveable furniture, whiteboards, good tech setup
- **Facilitation notes:** Plan for movement (standing, pairing, regrouping)

**Virtual (synchronous):**
- **Advantages:** Accessible to more faculty, easier to record, flexible scheduling
- **Logistics:** Use breakout rooms, shared docs, polls, chat
- **Facilitation notes:** Plan for engagement (chat participation, visual slides, frequent breakouts); record sessions for those who miss

**Hybrid:**
- **Advantages:** Flexibility for participants, accommodates different needs
- **Logistics:** Requires excellent tech setup (good camera, mic, screen sharing)
- **Facilitation notes:** Actively include remote participants; assign a co-facilitator to monitor chat/virtual room

**Asynchronous components:**
- Use discussion boards for sharing experiments between sessions
- Post reflection prompts for participants to respond to at their own pace
- Create video walkthroughs of the experiment template
- **Best for:** Supporting large groups, accommodating busy schedules, extending learning between sessions

---

## Session-by-Session Facilitation Notes

### Session 1: Welcome & Design First Experiment

**Your goals:**
- Establish psychological safety
- Introduce sandbox philosophy through experience, not lecture
- Help participants design realistic first experiments
- Send them off with confidence (even if uncertain)

**Preparation:**
- [ ] Review participant pre-survey responses (if you collect them)
- [ ] Prepare 2-3 discipline-specific examples
- [ ] Set up physical/virtual space for paired work
- [ ] Print experiment templates (if in-person)
- [ ] Prepare opening remarks

**Facilitation tips:**

**Opening (10-15 min):**
- Welcome people individually as they arrive (names, small talk, ease in)
- Share your own curiosity and uncertainty—model not having all the answers
- Explicitly name that skepticism and worry are welcome

**Setting intentions (5-7 min):**
- Give real silence for writing—don't rush this
- Make sharing optional (call for volunteers, don't go around the circle)
- If someone shares a concern, thank them and normalize it—don't defend or dismiss

**Experiment template walkthrough (20-25 min):**
- Use a real example, not a hypothetical one
- Walk through each section slowly
- Point out what makes it a good sandbox experiment (small, reversible, documented)

**Individual design time (20-25 min):**
- Give structured prompts (Option A/B/C) to help people start
- Circulate and check in—answer questions, offer encouragement
- Watch for people who are stuck—offer to talk through it with them

**Paired feedback (10-15 min):**
- Be explicit about roles (listener and designer)
- Give time warnings (halfway, 2 minutes left)
- Offer sentence starters for giving feedback

**Closing (5-10 min):**
- Emphasize that NOT trying the experiment is also data—document why
- Remind them of support available between sessions
- End with encouragement, not pressure

---

### Session 2: Experiment Check-in & Iteration Workshop

**Your goals:**
- Normalize failure and mixed results
- Help participants reflect honestly on what happened
- Support iteration and troubleshooting
- Build confidence in the experimental process

**Preparation:**
- [ ] Review any between-session communication or questions
- [ ] Prepare troubleshooting examples
- [ ] Set up small groups (3-4 people, mixed disciplines if possible)
- [ ] Have backup activities ready if most people didn't try experiments yet

**Facilitation tips:**

**Opening pulse check:**
- Use the green/yellow/red/white framework
- Count hands for each color
- Acknowledge the distribution without judgment ("Lots of yellows and reds—that's perfect for learning")

**Individual reflection time (5-7 min):**
- Protect this quiet time—it's essential for honest self-assessment
- Resist the urge to fill silence

**Small group sharing (25-35 min):**
- Give clear time structure (7-10 min per person)
- Circulate and listen—don't intervene unless asked
- Notice themes emerging across groups

**Whole-group troubleshooting (20-30 min):**
- After small groups, ask: "What challenges came up in your groups?"
- Invite participants to share challenges and crowdsource solutions
- Use the "common challenges" section as a resource, but prioritize real examples from the room

**Iteration planning (10-15 min):**
- Give structured time for participants to decide: iterate, pivot, or pause
- Emphasize that all three options are legitimate

---

### Session 3: Expanding Your Practice

**Your goals:**
- Deepen the questions beyond surface-level "does it work"
- Address pedagogical and ethical considerations
- Support participants in designing more ambitious (but still small) experiments
- Build toward sustained inquiry

**Preparation:**
- [ ] Review themes from previous sessions
- [ ] Prepare examples that illustrate deeper questions (equity, learning, values)
- [ ] Set up learning stations or breakouts (if using that format)
- [ ] Have discussion prompts ready for ethical and pedagogical questions

**Facilitation tips:**

**Opening patterns discussion (10-15 min):**
- Use a whiteboard or shared doc to capture themes
- Ask: "What patterns are you noticing across your experiments?"
- Reflect themes back to the group—this builds collective knowledge

**Deepening questions (20-25 min):**
- Don't lecture—use prompts and examples to open discussion
- Give space for disagreement and ambiguity
- Lean into questions that don't have easy answers (equity, student learning)

**Values reflection (5-7 min):**
- This is personal work—protect quiet time
- Make sharing optional

**Second experiment design (20-25 min):**
- Participants already know the template—they need less guidance now
- Circulate and ask questions: "How does this build on what you learned?" "What's different this time?"

**Peer consultation (15-20 min):**
- Use pairs or small groups
- Encourage participants to challenge each other thoughtfully

---

### Session 4: Reflection, Sharing & Sustaining

**Your goals:**
- Honor the work participants have done
- Help participants articulate their learning
- Build a plan for continued practice
- Create closure and celebrate growth

**Preparation:**
- [ ] Decide on sharing format (showcase vs. learning stations)
- [ ] Prepare options for ongoing connection (check-in schedule, discussion space, etc.)
- [ ] Have final evaluation survey ready
- [ ] Bring closing ceremony energy (this matters!)

**Facilitation tips:**

**Opening reflection (10-15 min):**
- Give everyone a voice in the opening round
- Expect emotion—transformation is happening here
- Hold space for people to name growth and change

**Individual reflection time (10-12 min):**
- This is their documentation for future reference—give real time
- Resist rushing this section

**Gallery of experiments (30-40 min):**
- Choose format based on group size (showcase for small, stations for large)
- Celebrate what people tried, not just what "worked"
- Make space for questions and learning from each other

**Continuation planning (15-20 min):**
- Be concrete about what support is available
- Ask participants what they need to sustain this
- Offer options for ongoing connection—gauge interest, commit to what's realistic

**Closing ceremony (10-15 min):**
- Make this meaningful—this is where you honor the journey
- Public commitments create accountability (but make them optional)
- End with gratitude and acknowledgment

## Facilitation Skills & Mindset

### Creating Psychological Safety

**Psychological safety** is the foundation of this workshop. Without it, faculty won't share honestly, take risks, or admit when things don't work.

**How to build it:**

✅ **Model vulnerability** - Share your own uncertainties, mistakes, and questions
✅ **Normalize failure** - Celebrate experiments that didn't work as valuable learning
✅ **Make sharing optional** - Never force people to speak; invite volunteers
✅ **Respond without judgment** - Thank people for sharing, especially when it's hard
✅ **Name discomfort** - Acknowledge when topics are controversial or uncomfortable
✅ **Protect confidentiality** - What's shared in the workshop stays in the workshop (unless someone gives permission to share)

**What undermines it:**

- ❌ Dismissing concerns or skepticism
- ❌ Positioning yourself as the expert with all the answers
- ❌ Comparing participants' progress to each other
- ❌ Rushing through reflection or sharing time
- ❌ Letting one person dominate discussion
- ❌ Failing to address harmful comments (e.g., dismissive remarks about students or colleagues)

---

### Asking Good Questions

**Your job is not to answer questions—it's to ask them.**

**Good facilitation questions:**

**For opening up thinking:**
- "What are you noticing?"
- "What surprised you?"
- "What's making you curious?"
- "What would you try if you knew it would work?"

**For deepening reflection:**
- "What does that tell you about...?"
- "How does this connect to what you value in your teaching?"
- "What assumptions are you holding?"
- "What would have to be true for this to work?"

**For supporting peers:**
- "What questions does this raise for you?"
- "What resonates with your experience?"
- "What would you be curious to try based on what you just heard?"

**For troubleshooting:**
- "What part of this is in your control?"
- "What would make this easier or more realistic?"
- "What's the smallest version of this you could try?"

---

### Navigating Difficult Moments

**Challenge: Someone is very skeptical or resistant**

**What to do:**
- Acknowledge their concerns as legitimate
- Ask: "What would need to be true for you to feel differently?" or "What worries you most?"
- Emphasize that experiments are optional and reversible
- Pair them with someone who shares their concerns but is trying things anyway

**What NOT to do:**
- Try to convince them or defend AI
- Dismiss their concerns as uninformed
- Force them to participate in activities they're uncomfortable with

---

**Challenge: Someone dominates discussion**

**What to do:**
- Use structured turn-taking ("Let's hear from someone who hasn't spoken yet")
- Break into smaller groups where one person can't dominate
- Speak privately: "I appreciate your contributions—can you help me make space for quieter voices?"

**What NOT to do:**
- Let it continue unchecked
- Embarrass them publicly

---

**Challenge: Someone shares something problematic (e.g., using AI in ways that harm students)**

**What to do:**
- Pause and name the concern: "I want to pause here—this raises some questions for me about..."
- Invite the group: "What concerns does this raise for you?"
- Redirect to principles: "How does this align with our principle of transparency/responsibility?"

**What NOT to do:**
- Let it pass without comment (silence = endorsement)
- Attack the person

---

**Challenge: Most people didn't try their experiments**

**What to do:**
- Normalize it: "This is common—trying new things is hard"
- Explore why: "What got in the way?"
- Pivot to observation: "What did you notice in your teaching this week, even if you didn't run the experiment?"
- Refine experiments: "What would make this more doable?"

**What NOT to do:**
- Guilt or shame
- Skip reflection and just move on

---

**Challenge: The conversation gets too abstract or theoretical**

**What to do:**
- Bring it back to practice: "What would this look like in your classroom next week?"
- Ask for examples: "Can someone share a concrete example?"
- Redirect to the experiment template: "How would you document this as an experiment?"

---

## Between-Session Support

**Your goal:** Keep momentum without overwhelming participants.

### Communication Strategy

**What to send:**

**Immediately after Session 1:**
- Thank you email
- Link to experiment template (digital version)
- Reminder of when next session is
- Your contact info for questions

**Mid-week check-in (optional):**
- Brief encouragement
- One troubleshooting tip
- Reminder that they can reach out

**Before next session (2 days):**
- Reminder of date/time/location
- What to bring (their experiment notes)
- One reflection prompt to prepare

**What NOT to do:**
- Send daily emails (too much)
- Make them feel guilty if they haven't tried experiments yet
- Add new requirements or homework

---

### Handling Questions Between Sessions

**Common questions you'll receive:**

**"Is this okay to try?"**
- Respond: "What do you think? What risks are you concerned about?"
- Help them think through risks and mitigations, don't just say yes or no

**"I'm stuck—can you help?"**
- Respond: "Tell me more about where you're stuck"
- Ask questions to help them problem-solve; offer to discuss by phone/video if needed

**"Do I have to try the experiment before Session 2?"**
- Respond: "It's valuable if you do, but if you don't, we'll use Session 2 to figure out why and refine the plan. Bring your thinking either way."

## Evaluation & Iteration

**How to know if the workshop is working:**

✅ **Participation:** Are people showing up and staying engaged?
✅ **Experimentation:** Are people trying things, even if they fail?
✅ **Honesty:** Are people sharing real challenges, not just successes?
✅ **Peer learning:** Are participants learning from each other, not just from you?
✅ **Sustained inquiry:** Are people continuing to experiment after the workshop ends?

**Red flags:**

🚨 People stop showing up after Session 1
🚨 No one is trying experiments
🚨 Discussion is superficial or performative
🚨 One person or perspective dominates
🚨 Participants look to you for all answers

---

### After Each Session: Reflect & Adjust

**Ask yourself:**
- What went well?
- What felt off?
- What would I change next time?
- What surprised me?
- Did everyone have a voice?
- What themes emerged?

**Adjust next session based on:**
- What participants actually tried (not what you expected)
- Emerging questions or concerns
- Energy level and engagement
- Time—what took longer or shorter than planned?

## FAQs for Facilitators

**Q: Do I need to be an AI expert to facilitate this workshop?**

**A:** No. You need to be curious, humble, and good at facilitating peer learning. Participants will teach each other (and you) about AI.

---

**Q: What if participants ask technical questions I can't answer?**

**A:** Say "I don't know—does anyone in the group have experience with this?" or "That's a great question—let's add it to our list to explore." You're facilitating a learning community, not delivering expert training.

---

**Q: What if my institution has restrictive AI policies?**

**A:** Acknowledge the constraints. Focus on experiments within policy boundaries, or frame this as "policy-compliant experimentation." If policies are unclear, use the workshop to collectively surface questions and advocate for clearer guidance.

---

**Q: How do I handle participants with very different levels of AI experience?**

**A:** Pair experienced participants with novices. Position experienced users as resources, not experts. Emphasize that everyone is experimenting and learning, regardless of technical skill.

---

**Q: What if the workshop is too short/long for my context?**

**A:** Adapt! See the timeline variations section above. The core is: design experiment → try it → reflect → iterate. You can compress or expand that cycle based on your constraints.

---

**Q: Can I run this workshop alone, or do I need a co-facilitator?**

**A:** You can run it alone for groups up to 15. For larger groups, a co-facilitator is very helpful for managing breakouts, monitoring chat (if virtual), and providing individual support.

---

## Final Thoughts for Facilitators

**This workshop is as much for you as it is for participants.** You'll learn about AI, about your institution, about what faculty need. Stay curious. Stay humble. Trust the process.

**Your most important job is to create the container** where people feel safe to try, fail, and share. Everything else is secondary.

**You don't need to have all the answers.** Model what it looks like to experiment, learn, and adjust. That's the practice you're teaching.

Good luck. You've got this.

120:T5820,# Facilitator Guide: Running the Sandbox Workshop

## About This Guide

This guide is for **you, the facilitator**—the person responsible for creating the conditions where faculty can experiment safely, reflect honestly, and learn from each other.

The workshop content (Sessions 1-4) is written as **live-flow material** for participants. This guide is the **meta-layer**: how to adapt, facilitate, and sustain the workshop in your specific context.

---

## Workshop Overview

### Philosophy & Approach

**This is not a training.** This is a **learning community** where faculty develop their own practice through experimentation.

**Core principles:**
- **Psychological safety** - Faculty need to feel safe trying things that might fail
- **Peer learning** - The best insights come from each other, not the facilitator
- **Practice-based** - Learning happens through doing, not just discussing
- **Iterative** - Small experiments, honest reflection, continuous adjustment
- **Contextual** - What works depends on discipline, students, and teaching style

**Your role as facilitator:**
- Create the container for safe experimentation
- Ask good questions
- Normalize failure as learning
- Connect participants to each other
- Model curiosity and humility

**Not your role:**
- Be an AI expert
- Have all the answers
- Fix everyone's problems
- Convince skeptics
- Police what people do

---

## Workshop Structure

### Recommended Format

**4 sessions over 4-6 weeks**

- **Session 1** (90 minutes): Welcome & Design First Experiment
- **Session 2** (60-90 minutes): Check-in & Iteration Workshop
- **Session 3** (60-90 minutes): Expanding Your Practice
- **Session 4** (90 minutes): Reflection, Sharing & Sustaining

**Between-session work:** 2-4 hours per week trying experiments

**Total time commitment:** ~20-25 hours over 4-6 weeks

---

### Why This Structure?

**Session 1 is longer** because participants need time to:
- Build trust and psychological safety
- Understand the sandbox philosophy
- Design their first experiment thoughtfully

**Sessions 2-3 can be shorter** because:
- Participants already understand the process
- Much of the learning happens between sessions
- Sessions are for sharing and troubleshooting, not lecturing

**Session 4 is longer** to create space for:
- Reflection on the full journey
- Sharing significant insights
- Planning for sustainability
- Closing with intention

---

## Adapting This Workshop

### What to Keep (Core Elements)

These are **non-negotiable** for maintaining the sandbox philosophy:

✅ **Psychological safety** - Make it safe to fail and share honestly
✅ **Small experiments** - No pressure to transform teaching overnight
✅ **Documentation** - Use the experiment template to capture observations
✅ **Reflection** - Regular opportunities to make meaning of experiences
✅ **Peer learning** - Participants learn from each other, not just from you
✅ **Iteration** - Experiments are starting points, not endpoints

---

### What to Customize (Flexible Elements)

◯ **Timeline** - Adjust to your academic calendar
◯ **Session length** - Shorten or extend based on your context
◯ **Group size** - Works for cohorts of 5-25 (see guidance below)
◯ **Format** - In-person, virtual, or hybrid
◯ **Examples** - Use discipline-specific examples relevant to your participants
◯ **Communication style** - Adapt tone and language to your institutional culture

---

## Context-Specific Adaptations

### Timeline Variations

**Intensive format** (2 weeks):
- 4 sessions over 2 weeks (2 sessions per week)
- Between-session work: 3-5 days
- **Best for:** Intersession periods, faculty development weeks
- **Challenge:** Less time for iteration; experiments may feel rushed

**Standard format** (4-6 weeks):
- 4 sessions over 4-6 weeks (1 session per week or biweekly)
- Between-session work: 1-2 weeks
- **Best for:** Regular semester, sustained community building
- **Challenge:** Maintaining momentum across longer gaps

**Extended format** (8-10 weeks):
- 4 sessions spread across a full semester
- Between-session work: 2-3 weeks
- **Best for:** Deep exploration, multiple iterations per experiment
- **Challenge:** Participants may lose continuity; need stronger between-session support

---

### Group Size Considerations

**Small groups** (5-10 participants):
- **Advantages:** More intimate sharing, deeper discussion, easier facilitation
- **Approach:** Whole-group discussions, paired feedback, minimal breakouts
- **Facilitation notes:** You can give more individual attention; surface everyone's voice

**Medium groups** (10-20 participants):
- **Advantages:** More diversity of experience, still manageable for connection
- **Approach:** Mix of whole-group and small-group activities, use breakout rooms
- **Facilitation notes:** Plan for structured small-group time; use breakouts strategically

**Large groups** (20-30+):
- **Advantages:** Reaches more faculty, more examples to learn from
- **Approach:** Use learning stations, breakout rooms, asynchronous components
- **Facilitation notes:** Consider co-facilitators; use async discussion boards between sessions; prioritize small-group interaction over whole-group sharing

---

### Format Options

**In-person:**
- **Advantages:** Easier to build trust, better for paired activities, more spontaneous discussion
- **Logistics:** Book accessible room with moveable furniture, whiteboards, good tech setup
- **Facilitation notes:** Plan for movement (standing, pairing, regrouping)

**Virtual (synchronous):**
- **Advantages:** Accessible to more faculty, easier to record, flexible scheduling
- **Logistics:** Use breakout rooms, shared docs, polls, chat
- **Facilitation notes:** Plan for engagement (chat participation, visual slides, frequent breakouts); record sessions for those who miss

**Hybrid:**
- **Advantages:** Flexibility for participants, accommodates different needs
- **Logistics:** Requires excellent tech setup (good camera, mic, screen sharing)
- **Facilitation notes:** Actively include remote participants; assign a co-facilitator to monitor chat/virtual room

**Asynchronous components:**
- Use discussion boards for sharing experiments between sessions
- Post reflection prompts for participants to respond to at their own pace
- Create video walkthroughs of the experiment template
- **Best for:** Supporting large groups, accommodating busy schedules, extending learning between sessions

## Session-by-Session Facilitation Notes

### Session 1: Welcome & Design First Experiment

**Your goals:**
- Establish psychological safety
- Introduce sandbox philosophy through experience, not lecture
- Help participants design realistic first experiments
- Send them off with confidence (even if uncertain)

**Preparation:**
- [ ] Review participant pre-survey responses (if you collect them)
- [ ] Prepare 2-3 discipline-specific examples
- [ ] Set up physical/virtual space for paired work
- [ ] Print experiment templates (if in-person)
- [ ] Prepare opening remarks

**Facilitation tips:**

**Opening (10-15 min):**
- Welcome people individually as they arrive (names, small talk, ease in)
- Share your own curiosity and uncertainty—model not having all the answers
- Explicitly name that skepticism and worry are welcome

**Setting intentions (5-7 min):**
- Give real silence for writing—don't rush this
- Make sharing optional (call for volunteers, don't go around the circle)
- If someone shares a concern, thank them and normalize it—don't defend or dismiss

**Experiment template walkthrough (20-25 min):**
- Use a real example, not a hypothetical one
- Walk through each section slowly
- Point out what makes it a good sandbox experiment (small, reversible, documented)

**Individual design time (20-25 min):**
- Give structured prompts (Option A/B/C) to help people start
- Circulate and check in—answer questions, offer encouragement
- Watch for people who are stuck—offer to talk through it with them

**Paired feedback (10-15 min):**
- Be explicit about roles (listener and designer)
- Give time warnings (halfway, 2 minutes left)
- Offer sentence starters for giving feedback

**Closing (5-10 min):**
- Emphasize that NOT trying the experiment is also data—document why
- Remind them of support available between sessions
- End with encouragement, not pressure

---

### Session 2: Experiment Check-in & Iteration Workshop

**Your goals:**
- Normalize failure and mixed results
- Help participants reflect honestly on what happened
- Support iteration and troubleshooting
- Build confidence in the experimental process

**Preparation:**
- [ ] Review any between-session communication or questions
- [ ] Prepare troubleshooting examples
- [ ] Set up small groups (3-4 people, mixed disciplines if possible)
- [ ] Have backup activities ready if most people didn't try experiments yet

**Facilitation tips:**

**Opening pulse check:**
- Use the green/yellow/red/white framework
- Count hands for each color
- Acknowledge the distribution without judgment ("Lots of yellows and reds—that's perfect for learning")

**Individual reflection time (5-7 min):**
- Protect this quiet time—it's essential for honest self-assessment
- Resist the urge to fill silence

**Small group sharing (25-35 min):**
- Give clear time structure (7-10 min per person)
- Circulate and listen—don't intervene unless asked
- Notice themes emerging across groups

**Whole-group troubleshooting (20-30 min):**
- After small groups, ask: "What challenges came up in your groups?"
- Invite participants to share challenges and crowdsource solutions
- Use the "common challenges" section as a resource, but prioritize real examples from the room

**Iteration planning (10-15 min):**
- Give structured time for participants to decide: iterate, pivot, or pause
- Emphasize that all three options are legitimate

---

### Session 3: Expanding Your Practice

**Your goals:**
- Deepen the questions beyond surface-level "does it work"
- Address pedagogical and ethical considerations
- Support participants in designing more ambitious (but still small) experiments
- Build toward sustained inquiry

**Preparation:**
- [ ] Review themes from previous sessions
- [ ] Prepare examples that illustrate deeper questions (equity, learning, values)
- [ ] Set up learning stations or breakouts (if using that format)
- [ ] Have discussion prompts ready for ethical and pedagogical questions

**Facilitation tips:**

**Opening patterns discussion (10-15 min):**
- Use a whiteboard or shared doc to capture themes
- Ask: "What patterns are you noticing across your experiments?"
- Reflect themes back to the group—this builds collective knowledge

**Deepening questions (20-25 min):**
- Don't lecture—use prompts and examples to open discussion
- Give space for disagreement and ambiguity
- Lean into questions that don't have easy answers (equity, student learning)

**Values reflection (5-7 min):**
- This is personal work—protect quiet time
- Make sharing optional

**Second experiment design (20-25 min):**
- Participants already know the template—they need less guidance now
- Circulate and ask questions: "How does this build on what you learned?" "What's different this time?"

**Peer consultation (15-20 min):**
- Use pairs or small groups
- Encourage participants to challenge each other thoughtfully

---

### Session 4: Reflection, Sharing & Sustaining

**Your goals:**
- Honor the work participants have done
- Help participants articulate their learning
- Build a plan for continued practice
- Create closure and celebrate growth

**Preparation:**
- [ ] Decide on sharing format (showcase vs. learning stations)
- [ ] Prepare options for ongoing connection (check-in schedule, discussion space, etc.)
- [ ] Have final evaluation survey ready
- [ ] Bring closing ceremony energy (this matters!)

**Facilitation tips:**

**Opening reflection (10-15 min):**
- Give everyone a voice in the opening round
- Expect emotion—transformation is happening here
- Hold space for people to name growth and change

**Individual reflection time (10-12 min):**
- This is their documentation for future reference—give real time
- Resist rushing this section

**Gallery of experiments (30-40 min):**
- Choose format based on group size (showcase for small, stations for large)
- Celebrate what people tried, not just what "worked"
- Make space for questions and learning from each other

**Continuation planning (15-20 min):**
- Be concrete about what support is available
- Ask participants what they need to sustain this
- Offer options for ongoing connection—gauge interest, commit to what's realistic

**Closing ceremony (10-15 min):**
- Make this meaningful—this is where you honor the journey
- Public commitments create accountability (but make them optional)
- End with gratitude and acknowledgment

## Facilitation Skills & Mindset

### Creating Psychological Safety

**Psychological safety** is the foundation of this workshop. Without it, faculty won't share honestly, take risks, or admit when things don't work.

**How to build it:**

✅ **Model vulnerability** - Share your own uncertainties, mistakes, and questions
✅ **Normalize failure** - Celebrate experiments that didn't work as valuable learning
✅ **Make sharing optional** - Never force people to speak; invite volunteers
✅ **Respond without judgment** - Thank people for sharing, especially when it's hard
✅ **Name discomfort** - Acknowledge when topics are controversial or uncomfortable
✅ **Protect confidentiality** - What's shared in the workshop stays in the workshop (unless someone gives permission to share)

**What undermines it:**

- ❌ Dismissing concerns or skepticism
- ❌ Positioning yourself as the expert with all the answers
- ❌ Comparing participants' progress to each other
- ❌ Rushing through reflection or sharing time
- ❌ Letting one person dominate discussion
- ❌ Failing to address harmful comments (e.g., dismissive remarks about students or colleagues)

---

### Asking Good Questions

**Your job is not to answer questions—it's to ask them.**

**Good facilitation questions:**

**For opening up thinking:**
- "What are you noticing?"
- "What surprised you?"
- "What's making you curious?"
- "What would you try if you knew it would work?"

**For deepening reflection:**
- "What does that tell you about...?"
- "How does this connect to what you value in your teaching?"
- "What assumptions are you holding?"
- "What would have to be true for this to work?"

**For supporting peers:**
- "What questions does this raise for you?"
- "What resonates with your experience?"
- "What would you be curious to try based on what you just heard?"

**For troubleshooting:**
- "What part of this is in your control?"
- "What would make this easier or more realistic?"
- "What's the smallest version of this you could try?"

---

### Navigating Difficult Moments

**Challenge: Someone is very skeptical or resistant**

**What to do:**
- Acknowledge their concerns as legitimate
- Ask: "What would need to be true for you to feel differently?" or "What worries you most?"
- Emphasize that experiments are optional and reversible
- Pair them with someone who shares their concerns but is trying things anyway

**What NOT to do:**
- Try to convince them or defend AI
- Dismiss their concerns as uninformed
- Force them to participate in activities they're uncomfortable with

---

**Challenge: Someone dominates discussion**

**What to do:**
- Use structured turn-taking ("Let's hear from someone who hasn't spoken yet")
- Break into smaller groups where one person can't dominate
- Speak privately: "I appreciate your contributions—can you help me make space for quieter voices?"

**What NOT to do:**
- Let it continue unchecked
- Embarrass them publicly

---

**Challenge: Someone shares something problematic (e.g., using AI in ways that harm students)**

**What to do:**
- Pause and name the concern: "I want to pause here—this raises some questions for me about..."
- Invite the group: "What concerns does this raise for you?"
- Redirect to principles: "How does this align with our principle of transparency/responsibility?"

**What NOT to do:**
- Let it pass without comment (silence = endorsement)
- Attack the person

---

**Challenge: Most people didn't try their experiments**

**What to do:**
- Normalize it: "This is common—trying new things is hard"
- Explore why: "What got in the way?"
- Pivot to observation: "What did you notice in your teaching this week, even if you didn't run the experiment?"
- Refine experiments: "What would make this more doable?"

**What NOT to do:**
- Guilt or shame
- Skip reflection and just move on

---

**Challenge: The conversation gets too abstract or theoretical**

**What to do:**
- Bring it back to practice: "What would this look like in your classroom next week?"
- Ask for examples: "Can someone share a concrete example?"
- Redirect to the experiment template: "How would you document this as an experiment?"

---

## Between-Session Support

**Your goal:** Keep momentum without overwhelming participants.

### Communication Strategy

**What to send:**

**Immediately after Session 1:**
- Thank you email
- Link to experiment template (digital version)
- Reminder of when next session is
- Your contact info for questions

**Mid-week check-in (optional):**
- Brief encouragement
- One troubleshooting tip
- Reminder that they can reach out

**Before next session (2 days):**
- Reminder of date/time/location
- What to bring (their experiment notes)
- One reflection prompt to prepare

**What NOT to do:**
- Send daily emails (too much)
- Make them feel guilty if they haven't tried experiments yet
- Add new requirements or homework

---

### Handling Questions Between Sessions

**Common questions you'll receive:**

**"Is this okay to try?"**
- Respond: "What do you think? What risks are you concerned about?"
- Help them think through risks and mitigations, don't just say yes or no

**"I'm stuck—can you help?"**
- Respond: "Tell me more about where you're stuck"
- Ask questions to help them problem-solve; offer to discuss by phone/video if needed

**"Do I have to try the experiment before Session 2?"**
- Respond: "It's valuable if you do, but if you don't, we'll use Session 2 to figure out why and refine the plan. Bring your thinking either way."

## Evaluation & Iteration

**How to know if the workshop is working:**

✅ **Participation:** Are people showing up and staying engaged?
✅ **Experimentation:** Are people trying things, even if they fail?
✅ **Honesty:** Are people sharing real challenges, not just successes?
✅ **Peer learning:** Are participants learning from each other, not just from you?
✅ **Sustained inquiry:** Are people continuing to experiment after the workshop ends?

**Red flags:**

🚨 People stop showing up after Session 1
🚨 No one is trying experiments
🚨 Discussion is superficial or performative
🚨 One person or perspective dominates
🚨 Participants look to you for all answers

---

### After Each Session: Reflect & Adjust

**Ask yourself:**
- What went well?
- What felt off?
- What would I change next time?
- What surprised me?
- Did everyone have a voice?
- What themes emerged?

**Adjust next session based on:**
- What participants actually tried (not what you expected)
- Emerging questions or concerns
- Energy level and engagement
- Time—what took longer or shorter than planned?

## FAQs for Facilitators

**Q: Do I need to be an AI expert to facilitate this workshop?**

**A:** No. You need to be curious, humble, and good at facilitating peer learning. Participants will teach each other (and you) about AI.

---

**Q: What if participants ask technical questions I can't answer?**

**A:** Say "I don't know—does anyone in the group have experience with this?" or "That's a great question—let's add it to our list to explore." You're facilitating a learning community, not delivering expert training.

---

**Q: What if my institution has restrictive AI policies?**

**A:** Acknowledge the constraints. Focus on experiments within policy boundaries, or frame this as "policy-compliant experimentation." If policies are unclear, use the workshop to collectively surface questions and advocate for clearer guidance.

---

**Q: How do I handle participants with very different levels of AI experience?**

**A:** Pair experienced participants with novices. Position experienced users as resources, not experts. Emphasize that everyone is experimenting and learning, regardless of technical skill.

---

**Q: What if the workshop is too short/long for my context?**

**A:** Adapt! See the timeline variations section above. The core is: design experiment → try it → reflect → iterate. You can compress or expand that cycle based on your constraints.

---

**Q: Can I run this workshop alone, or do I need a co-facilitator?**

**A:** You can run it alone for groups up to 15. For larger groups, a co-facilitator is very helpful for managing breakouts, monitoring chat (if virtual), and providing individual support.

---

## Final Thoughts for Facilitators

**This workshop is as much for you as it is for participants.** You'll learn about AI, about your institution, about what faculty need. Stay curious. Stay humble. Trust the process.

**Your most important job is to create the container** where people feel safe to try, fail, and share. Everything else is secondary.

**You don't need to have all the answers.** Model what it looks like to experiment, learn, and adjust. That's the practice you're teaching.

Good luck. You've got this.
