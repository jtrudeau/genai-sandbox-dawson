Completed Example: Revising Assignment AI-Use Instructions

1. Choose One Small Experiment

  • What this experiment is about: I want to rewrite the AI-use instructions on one assignment handout. Right now my instructions are generic and students keep asking what counts as "using AI" on this particular assignment. I want something specific enough that they know what is allowed, what is not, and what they need to disclose.
  • First use for this experiment: The next handout for a 600-word comparative reading response in my first-year English course. Students get the handout in week 8.
  • Who this is for: First-year English students. Most of them are already using AI in some form. They are not trying to cheat, but the current instructions leave too much room for interpretation.
  • What I will look for: Whether the revised instructions reduce the number of clarification emails I get, and whether student disclosure statements show they understood the boundaries.

2. Prepare the Experiment

  • What will I give the AI to work with? My current assignment handout with the generic AI statement, my rubric (especially the criteria on use of evidence and original analysis), and five student emails from last term asking what was and was not allowed. The assignment asks students to compare two short readings and build a focused argument with textual evidence. I will tell the AI that the goal is clearer instructions for students, not a rewrite of the assignment itself.
  • What will I check carefully myself? Whether the wording fits my course goals and department expectations. Whether it sounds punitive or implies rules the department has not actually set. Whether the language is plain enough that students will read it and understand what they need to do.

3. Run the Experiment

  • ☑ I can explain how AI was used.
  • ☑ I removed personal information or used a summary where needed.
  • ☑ I will review for accuracy, bias, and attribution.
  • Other notes: I summarized the student emails rather than pasting them directly. No student names or identifying details went into the tool.
  • What happened during the experiment? I pasted my handout, rubric criteria, and the summarized student questions into the conversation. I asked the AI to identify where my current wording was unclear based on the kinds of questions students were asking, and then draft a shorter version that separates what is allowed from what is not. The first draft was too permissive about paraphrasing and used confident policy language I had not approved, so I revised the prompt twice. I ended up rewriting about half of what the AI produced before I was satisfied with the tone.
  • Main output or result: A revised instructions paragraph and a short three-column box (allowed / not allowed / disclose) that replaces the old generic statement on the handout.
  • Other output or link: A short plain-language version I am considering for the LMS announcement that goes out with the handout.
  • Other files or links: Side-by-side notes comparing old and new wording, with my annotations on what changed and why.
  • Feedback or reactions: I showed the revised handout to a colleague who teaches another section. She said the distinction between using AI for brainstorming, outlining, and drafting was much clearer than before, and that the "disclose" column would save her time grading.

4. Review What Happened

  • What worked and what did not? The AI was useful for condensing repetitive wording and catching phrases that tried to do too much at once. Having the student questions in the conversation helped it focus on real confusion rather than hypothetical issues. What did not work: the first draft assumed a more permissive policy than my department actually has, and the tone was too formal for my students. I had to rewrite those parts myself. The side-by-side comparison I did afterward was more useful than the AI output on its own.
  • What would I change next time? I would start with the specific student questions rather than asking the AI to review the whole document at once. Leading with the confusion points gave better results than leading with the handout.

5. What Might Be Next

  • What might be next, if anything? Try the same approach on one peer-review task, where AI boundaries are different because the writing is collaborative. I want to see whether the three-column format works for a task where the lines are less clear.
  • What support would help? A review from a pedagogical adviser or department colleague who has thought about AI-use language in writing-intensive courses. I also want to check whether other sections of the course are using different instructions, since students notice inconsistencies. I could also use more practice giving the AI specific enough instructions that the output stays within my requirements. The first draft overstepped because the AI did not seem to know where the boundaries were.
