Completed Example: Revising Assignment AI-Use Instructions

Use your browser's print dialog to print this page or save it as a PDF.

1. Choose One Small Experiment

  • What this experiment is about: Revise the AI-use instructions for one short written assignment so students know what is allowed, what is not, and how to disclose use.
  • First use for this experiment: The next assignment handout for a 600-word comparative reading response in a first-year English course.
  • Who this is for: First-year English students.
  • What I will look for: Fewer clarification emails, clearer disclosure statements, and fewer confused uses of AI.

2. Prepare the Experiment

  • What I am bringing into the experiment: Current assignment handout, current generic AI statement, rubric criteria for use of evidence and concept application, and examples of student questions from last term.
  • What the AI needs to know: Students compare two short readings and build a focused written response with textual evidence. The goal is clearer instructions, not outsourcing student interpretation or writing.
  • What I will check carefully myself: Fit with course goals and department expectations. Whether the wording sounds punitive or implies rules the department has not approved. Whether students will actually read and understand the instructions.
  • ☑ I can explain how AI was used.
  • ☑ I removed personal information or used a summary where needed.
  • ☑ I will review for accuracy, bias, and attribution.
  • Other notes: Final wording still needs a quick check against department expectations.

3. Run the Experiment

  • What happened during the experiment: I asked AI to compare my current wording with the questions students asked last term and then draft a shorter version. I rewrote parts of the AI output before keeping any of it. The first draft sounded too confident about policy language and was too permissive about paraphrasing, so I revised twice.
  • Main output or result: Revised assignment paragraph plus a short "allowed / not allowed / disclose" box.
  • Other output or link: A short LMS post that restates the instructions in plain language.
  • Other files or links: Comparison notes between old wording and new wording.
  • Feedback or reactions: One colleague said the distinction between brainstorming, outlining, and drafting was much clearer in the revised version.

4. Review What Happened

  • What seemed useful: AI helped condense repetitive wording and surface phrases that were doing too much at once. The side-by-side comparison made it easier to see where the original was unclear.
  • What still needs work or checking: Department alignment, whether students understand "disclose use," and whether the final wording is short enough to read.
  • What I would change next time: Start with the specific student questions from last term instead of asking AI to review the whole document at once.

5. What Might Be Next

  • What might be next, if anything: Test the same style of AI-use guidance on one peer-review task and compare the questions students ask.
  • Support that would help: A quick review from a department colleague or pedagogical adviser.
  • Who I might share this with: The course team and department colleagues working on assignment guidance.
